Key takeaways

BMI is worked out the same way at every age: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, or pounds × 703 ÷ inches². From age 20, the categories don’t change with age: 18.5 to 24.9 is a healthy weight at 25 and at 75, 25 to 29.9 is overweight and 30 or more is obesity (CDC). Under 20, BMI is read as a percentile for age and sex.

  • What changes with age is the comparison. The median BMI of US women is 27.1 at ages 20 to 39, 29.3 at 40 to 59 and 28.4 at 60 and over; of US men, 27.3, 29.0 and 28.4 (CDC measurements, 2021–2023). The calculator ranks you within your own age group.
  • The average is above the healthy range: in every decade from the 20s to 80 and over, the average BMI of US women and of US men is 27.7 or more, in the overweight range.
  • There is no separate range for seniors. The 23 to 29.9 that some senior calculators call normal isn’t CDC’s; the meta-analysis they cite describes risk of death, not a healthy range.
  • Muscle mass tends to fall with age, so the same BMI can mean more body fat; the American Diabetes Association notes that this may lead to obesity being missed in older adults.

Medical disclaimer: This calculator gives estimates for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

How to read your result

Every number below uses the calculator’s starting values: a 45-year-old woman, 5′6″ (167.6 cm) and 170 lb (77.1 kg).

BMI: 27.4, overweight
170 × 703 ÷ 66² = 27.4, rounded to one decimal (CDC formula); the category comes from that rounded number. CDC’s adult cut-offs are the same at every age from 20: under 18.5 underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 healthy, 25 to 29.9 overweight, 30 and above obesity.
Healthy weight at 5′6″: 115–154 lb (52–70 kg)
The lightest and heaviest whole pounds at that height with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, and the same in whole kilograms. The range depends on height only, never on age.
Compared with US women aged 40 to 59: higher than 39%
About 39% of women aged 40 to 59, measured by CDC in 2021–2023, have a lower BMI and about 60% a higher one. Her BMI is under the median for her age group, 29.3, and still overweight. With the same height and weight at 25 she would rank higher than about 51% of women aged 20 to 39, and at 70 higher than about 43% of women 60 and over; the category stays overweight at all three ages. A man of 45 with the same numbers ranks higher than about 38% of men aged 40 to 59.
US women aged 40 to 49
Their average BMI is 30.7, their average weight 177.3 lb (80.4 kg) and their average waist 38.7 in (98.2 cm), CDC’s figures for her decade of age. An average describes US adults; it isn’t a target.
Weight milestones
The weight where each range starts at 5′6″: healthy from 115 lb, overweight from 155 lb, obesity from 186 lb, class 2 obesity from 217 lb and class 3 from 248 lb.
BMI Prime 1.10 and ponderal index 16.4 kg/m³
BMI Prime is your BMI divided by 25, where overweight starts. The ponderal index divides weight in kilograms by height in meters cubed; CDC’s adult categories use BMI, not this index.

BMI by age group: US women and men

CDC’s National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) weighed and measured 5,929 adults aged 20 and over from August 2021 to August 2023, pregnant women excluded. In every age group the median BMI was above 25: for women 27.1 at ages 20 to 39, 29.3 at 40 to 59 and 28.4 at 60 and over; for men 27.3, 29.0 and 28.4. The calculator ranks your BMI within your own group.

What changes with age, and what doesn’t

The cut-offs don’t change. For everyone 20 and older, CDC bases the adult categories on BMI “regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC), so a BMI of 25.0 is overweight at 25 and at 85.

Body composition does. Muscle mass decreases by about 3–8% per decade after age 30, and faster after 60 (Volpi et al., 2004), so the same BMI can mean more fat later in life. On CDC’s body scans, average body fat was 25.4% for men in their 20s and 28.3% in their 50s, and 36.9% and 40.3% for women (our calculation from NHANES 2011–2018; both differences are larger than their margins of error). These scans covered ages 8 to 59 only, so they don’t show older adults.

Height can change. BMI divides by height squared, so if your height drops, your BMI rises at the same weight: going from 5′8″ to 5′6″ adds about 1.5 points at a BMI of 25. Use your height as measured now.

The US averages differ by age. Average BMI was higher in the 40s and 50s than in the 20s, and lower at 80 and over than in the 50s, for women and for men (each difference is larger than its margin of error). These are different people measured in the same years, not the same people followed as they aged.

BMI at 65 and over

Some senior BMI calculators online class a BMI of 23 to 29.9 (or 23 to 30) as normal for adults over 65 and list the meta-analysis below among their sources. That range isn’t CDC’s: its adult categories have no upper age limit. In that meta-analysis of 32 studies covering 197,940 people aged 65 and over, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014): an association, not a healthy range or a target.

US adults aged 70 to 79 have a median BMI of 28.0 (women) and 28.8 (men), and 25.7% of the women and 20.9% of the men have a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 (our calculation). The American Diabetes Association adds a caution for this age: “Loss of muscle mass in older adults reduces the accuracy of BMI, which may lead to underdiagnosis of obesity” (ADA, 2026). A waist measurement shows what BMI can’t: the waist-to-height ratio guide explains how to take it.

Under 20: BMI-for-age percentiles

Children and teens get the same formula but not the adult categories. CDC compares their BMI with growth charts for the same age and sex and reads it as a percentile: under the 5th is underweight, the 5th to below the 85th healthy weight, the 85th to below the 95th overweight, and the 95th or above obesity (CDC). The same number means different things at different ages: a BMI of 23.6 is in the adult healthy range, but for a 10-year-old girl it sits at the 96th percentile, which is obesity.

This calculator starts at 20. For ages 2 to 19, the BMI calculator for children and teens takes the age in years and months.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 demographics and body measures files (DEMO_L, BMX_L): our calculation for 3,249 women and 2,680 men aged 20 and over (BMI percentiles by age group and by decade, shares in the healthy range).
  • Fryar CD, et al. Anthropometric Reference Data for Children and Adults: United States, August 2021–August 2023 (NCHS, Vital and Health Statistics Series 3, No. 50, 2025): the average BMI, weight and waist by decade.
  • Emmerich SD, et al. Obesity and Severe Obesity Prevalence in Adults: United States, August 2021–August 2023 (NCHS Data Brief No. 508, 2024): obesity by age group.
  • CDC NHANES 2011–2018 whole-body DXA scans: our calculation of average body fat by decade, ages 20 to 59 (the body fat percentage chart has the method).
  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the cut-offs and the “regardless of age, sex, or race” rule.
  • CDC. Calculating BMI: the formula in US and metric units.
  • CDC. Child and Teen BMI Categories: percentiles under 20.
  • Winter JE, et al. BMI and all-cause mortality in older adults: a meta-analysis (Am J Clin Nutr, 2014).
  • Volpi E, et al. Muscle tissue changes with aging (Curr Opin Clin Nutr Metab Care, 2004).
  • American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee for Obesity. Screening, Diagnosis, Evaluation, and Staging of Obesity in Adults: Standards of Care in Overweight and Obesity—2026: the note on older adults.

Method. Adults aged 20 and over with a measured height and weight in NHANES August 2021–August 2023, pregnant women excluded, weighted with CDC’s exam weights. Percentiles come from the weighted distribution of the measured BMIs (one weight per distinct BMI, straight lines in between), the method that reproduces NCHS’s published medians; the rank reads your BMI off the 1st to 99th percentiles of your sex and age group (20 to 39, 40 to 59, 60 and over), stored to four decimals. Shares and means carry 95% confidence intervals from Taylor linearization over the survey’s 15 strata, and the page states a difference between ages only when its interval excludes zero. NHANES records ages over 79 as 80, so the last decade is 80 and over. Every figure is rounded once, from the unrounded estimate. The calculator takes ages 20 and over; at 2 to 19 it sends you to the children’s calculator.
