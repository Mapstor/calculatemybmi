BMI Calculator for Kids and Teens

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Enter your child’s age, sex, height and weight to get their BMI-for-age percentile and CDC weight category.

For ages 2 through 19, with CDC’s BMI-for-age growth charts. Method and sources

—
—
—
Key takeaways

A child’s BMI is worked out the same way as an adult’s, then compared with CDC’s BMI-for-age charts for the child’s sex and age in months. The percentile sets the category: under the 5th is underweight, the 5th to below the 85th healthy weight, the 85th to below the 95th overweight, and the 95th or above obesity.

  • Severe obesity is a BMI at or above 120% of the 95th percentile, or 35 or more. The American Academy of Pediatrics splits it into class 2 (120% to under 140%) and class 3 (140% or more).
  • The same BMI means different things at different ages: 23.6 is in the adult healthy range, but for a 10-year-old girl it sits at the 96th percentile, which is obesity.
  • Up to the 95th percentile the charts are CDC’s 2000 growth charts; above it, CDC’s 2022 extended charts give percentiles up to the 99.99th.
  • In CDC’s measured survey for August 2021–August 2023, 21.1% of children and teens aged 2–19 had obesity.

Medical disclaimer: This calculator gives estimates for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

CDC BMI categories for children and teens

The same five categories apply from age 2 through 19.

BMI categories for children and teens aged 2 through 19 (CDC)
CategoryPercentile
UnderweightLess than the 5th percentile
Healthy weight5th percentile to less than the 85th percentile
Overweight85th to less than the 95th percentile
Obesity95th percentile or greater
Severe obesity120% of the 95th percentile or greater, or a BMI of 35 or greater

CDC adds that “the American Academy of Pediatrics uses an expanded definition of severe obesity,” in two classes:

  • Class 2 obesity: 120% to less than 140% of the 95th percentile, or a BMI of 35 to less than 40.
  • Class 3 obesity: 140% of the 95th percentile or greater, or a BMI of 40 or greater.

The calculator shows CDC’s category, and for severe obesity it adds the class on the line under it. CDC also notes: “Race is not a factor used in calculating BMI percentiles.”

How the calculator works

It works out BMI with CDC’s formula: 703 × weight in pounds ÷ height in inches², or weight in kilograms ÷ height in meters². Then it reads the child’s row in CDC’s data file for the extended BMI-for-age growth charts, which lists the values for boys and girls month by month. CDC explains how a row covers the month: “Age is listed at the half month point for the entire month; for example, 48.5 months represents 48.0 months up to but not including 49.0 months of age.” So a child of 2 years 0 months reads the 24.5-month row, and one of 9 years 6 months the 114.5-month row. The calculator takes any age from 24 to 239 months.

At or below the row’s 95th percentile, the percentile comes from the LMS formula, with the row’s L, M and S: z = ((BMI ÷ M)L − 1) ÷ (L × S), and the percentile is Φ(z) × 100, where Φ is the cumulative standard normal distribution. CDC writes that “BMI percentiles and z-scores up to the 95th percentile (z-score 1.645) are the same as those in the 2000 CDC BMI-for-age growth charts.”

Above the 95th, the extended charts use the row’s 95th percentile (P95) and its sigma: percentile = 90 + 10 × Φ((BMI − P95) ÷ sigma), and the z-score is Φ−1 of that percentile ÷ 100. That is how they add percentiles “above the 95th percentile up to the 99.99th percentile (z-scores up to 5).”

The calculator also gives BMI as a percentage of the 95th percentile, 100 × BMI ÷ P95. Severe obesity starts at 120%, and the American Academy of Pediatrics’ class 3 at 140%.

BMI is a screening measure. CDC notes that “BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass.”

Four worked examples

The first two are CDC’s own examples from its data file page. We typed the other two into the calculator above.

CDC’s example: a girl of 9 years 6 months, BMI 21.2

Her row is 114.5 months, where the 95th percentile is 22.3979. Her BMI is below it, so the LMS formula applies. CDC works it out to a z-score of 1.4215, the 92.2nd percentile. That is between the 85th and the 95th, so she is in the overweight category; the calculator shows the 92nd percentile and z = 1.42.

CDC’s example: a boy of 4 years 2 months, BMI 22.6

His row is 50.5 months: the 95th percentile is 17.8219 and sigma 2.398288. His BMI is above the 95th, so the extended formula applies: (22.6 − 17.8219) ÷ 2.398288 = 1.9923, Φ(1.9923) = 0.97683, and 90 + 10 × 0.97683 = 99.7683, the 99.7683rd percentile. A BMI of 22.6 is 126.8% of his row’s 95th percentile, over the 120% line, so the category is severe obesity, which the American Academy of Pediatrics calls class 2 obesity (120% to under 140%). The calculator shows it as above the 99th percentile.

A 10-year-old girl, 4′6″ and 98 lb

Her BMI is 703 × 98 ÷ 54² = 23.63, shown as 23.6. At 10 years 0 months her row is 120.5 months, where the 95th percentile is 22.9826 (23.0 in the table) and sigma 4.449948. Her BMI is above the 95th, so the extended formula applies: 90 + 10 × Φ((23.63 − 22.9826) ÷ 4.449948) gives 95.6, the 96th percentile, which is obesity. Her BMI is 102.8% of the 95th percentile, short of the 120% that would make it severe obesity.

A boy of 14 years 6 months, 5′5″ and 150 lb

His BMI is 703 × 150 ÷ 65² = 24.96, shown as 25.0. His row is 174.5 months, with a 95th percentile of 26.4509, so the LMS formula applies with the row’s L = −2.1794, M = 19.5067 and S = 0.1353: z = ((24.96 ÷ 19.5067)−2.1794 − 1) ÷ (−2.1794 × 0.1353) = 1.41, a percentile of 92.1. The calculator shows the 92nd percentile, in the overweight category.

How BMI changes with age

CDC gives the reason children are measured against their own age and sex: “Because children and teens are growing, their BMI values are expressed relative to others of the same sex and age. These values are called BMI-for-age percentiles.”

The median shows how far BMI moves. For boys it is 16.5 at age 2, falls to 15.4 at 5 and 6, and climbs to 22.5 at 19. For girls it is 16.4 at 2, 15.2 at 5 and 6, and 21.6 at 19.

A single cut-off can’t follow that: a BMI of 17.0 is above the 85th percentile for a 5-year-old girl (16.8), so it is overweight, but below the 5th for a 19-year-old (17.8), so it is underweight.

How common obesity is in children

In CDC’s measured survey for August 2021–August 2023, 21.1% of children and teens aged 2–19 had obesity and 7.0% had severe obesity (NCHS Health E-Stat 112). The childhood obesity statistics page has the trend and the figures by age and sex.

Method. The tables, the figure and the examples come from CDC’s data file, bmi-age-2022.csv, used unchanged; the calculator holds the same values to 8 significant digits, which changes no number shown here. BMI is CDC’s formula (US units × 703, or kg/m²), shown to 0.1. The calculator reads the row for the child’s month of age, the whole months plus 0.5, so 2 years 0 months is the 24.5-month row. At or below the row’s 95th percentile it uses the LMS formula; above it, CDC’s extended formula with the row’s sigma. Severe obesity is 120% of the row’s 95th percentile or a BMI of 35. The tables give the row that starts at each birthday (age × 12 + 0.5 months), with CDC’s percentile columns rounded half up to 0.1; the figure plots every month from 24.5 to 239.5.
