Lean Body Mass Calculator

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Estimate your lean body mass (everything except fat: muscles, bones, organs, water) and body fat percentage using the Boer formula.

Unlike standard BMI, which only considers height and weight, lean body mass separates your weight into two meaningful components: fat mass and everything else. BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass (CDC), so a muscular athlete and a sedentary person can share a BMI score and differ in how much of their weight is fat. The calculator below estimates lean body mass from height, weight and sex with the Boer formula; a body scan such as DXA measures it. Body composition is one of the health-related components of physical fitness, alongside cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance and flexibility (Caspersen et al., Public Health Reports 1985).

Body Fat Percentage vs BMI

Body fat percentage is the proportion of your total weight that is fat tissue. In CDC’s body scans, the same BMI corresponds to different body fat in men and women and at different ages (our analysis; see the body fat percentage chart), so the same BMI can mean quite different body compositions.

Comparing your body fat with what is typical for your age and sex tells you more than a BMI category alone. Someone classified as "overweight" by BMI may actually have an athletic body fat percentage if they carry significant muscle mass, as we explain in our body fat vs. BMI comparison.

The Boer Formula Explained

This calculator uses the Boer formula, published by P. Boer in 1984, to estimate lean body mass from height and weight. Boer used it to normalise body fluid volumes: blood volume, measured in 66 healthy adults, and extracellular fluid volume, measured in 54, lined up with estimated lean body mass the same way in men and women, and none of the other indices he tried (weight, height, body surface area, a leanness index) did (Boer, 1984).

The Formulas

For Men:

LBM = 0.407 x weight(kg) + 0.267 x height(cm) - 19.2

For Women:

LBM = 0.252 x weight(kg) + 0.473 x height(cm) - 48.3

Worked Example: Male, 5'10", 180 lbs

First, convert to metric: 180 lbs = 81.65 kg, 5'10" = 177.8 cm.

LBM = 0.407 x 81.65 + 0.267 x 177.8 - 19.2
LBM = 33.23 + 47.47 - 19.2
LBM = 61.50 kg (135.6 lbs)
Fat Mass = 81.65 - 61.50 = 20.14 kg (44.4 lbs), subtracted before rounding
Body Fat % = 20.14 / 81.65 x 100 = 24.7%

Worked Example: Female, 5'5", 140 lbs

Convert to metric: 140 lbs = 63.5 kg, 5'5" = 165.1 cm.

LBM = 0.252 x 63.5 + 0.473 x 165.1 - 48.3
LBM = 16.0 + 78.1 - 48.3
LBM = 45.8 kg (101.0 lbs)
Fat Mass = 63.5 - 45.8 = 17.7 kg (39.0 lbs)
Body Fat % = 17.7 / 63.5 x 100 = 27.9%

How the Two Boer Equations Differ

Each sex has its own equation. In the women’s, the height coefficient (0.473) is larger than the weight coefficient (0.252); in the men’s, the weight coefficient (0.407) is larger than the height coefficient (0.267).

Other LBM Formulas: A Comparison

Several formulas estimate lean body mass from height and weight. Boer’s, which this calculator uses, James’s and Hume’s are below, with what each gives for the same five people.

Boer Formula (1984)

Used in this calculator. Published by P. Boer in 1984 in the American Journal of Physiology; the equations below are as Caruso et al. (2018) print them, citing Boer.

  • Men: LBM = 0.407 x W + 0.267 x H - 19.2
  • Women: LBM = 0.252 x W + 0.473 x H - 48.3

James Formula (1976)

From the 1976 report of the DHSS/MRC Group on Obesity Research (Waterlow and James), as Caruso et al. (2018) print it. It squares the weight-to-height ratio, so at high weights its estimate rises more slowly, then stops rising and falls.

  • Men: LBM = 1.1 x W - 128 x (W/H)^2
  • Women: LBM = 1.07 x W - 148 x (W/H)^2

Hume Formula (1966)

Published by R. Hume in 1966 in the Journal of Clinical Pathology (Hume, 1966). Like Boer’s, it is linear in height and weight, with different coefficients. Hume fitted it to 29 men aged 40 to 77 and 27 women aged 37 to 80, among them patients with polycythaemia, chronic bronchitis or obesity, with lean body mass worked out from total body water.

  • Men: LBM = 0.3281 x W + 0.33929 x H - 29.5336
  • Women: LBM = 0.29569 x W + 0.41813 x H - 43.2933

Why Lean Body Mass Matters

Lean body mass matters for resting energy use, some drug doses and muscular athletes, and when it is measured it shows differences in build that BMI alone can’t.

1. Metabolic Rate and Calorie Burning

At rest, skeletal muscle uses about 13 kcal per kilogram a day and fat tissue about 4.5, figures from Elia that Wang and colleagues found held across adulthood (Wang et al., 2010). A kilogram of muscle at rest uses about 2.9 times as much as a kilogram of fat. Our BMI and metabolism guide has more.

2. Drug Dosing and Medical Applications

Some doses are worked out from lean body weight rather than total weight. Caruso and colleagues, for example, compared the Boer and James formulas for tailoring iodinated contrast injection to lean body weight in patients with obesity (Caruso et al., 2018). Which weight a dose is based on depends on the drug, so it is the prescriber’s decision.

3. Muscular Athletes

BMI can’t tell muscle from fat, so very muscular people can reach an obese BMI with little body fat. At the NFL Scouting Combine from 2010 to 2016, BMI classed 53.4% of the athletes as obese, against 8.9% by measured body fat (Provencher et al., 2018). Our BMI for athletes guide explains why BMI falls short for muscular people and what to use instead.

4. Same BMI, Different Build

Take two men, both 5'10" and 190 lbs, one at 15% body fat (LBM of 161.5 lbs) and one at 30% (LBM of 133 lbs). BMI puts both at 27.3, overweight. If both are 20 to 39 years old, Gallagher and colleagues’ provisional ranges put 15% body fat within the range that matches a healthy BMI (8 to 19.9%) and 30% above the 25% that matches a BMI of 30 (Gallagher et al., 2000). The Boer formula can’t see that difference: from height, weight and sex alone, it gives both men 139.7 lbs of lean mass and 26.5% body fat. Our BMI vs. body composition guide has more.

Methods to Measure Body Composition

The reference is a DXA scan, which CDC’s survey documentation calls “the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition”. Cheaper methods read differently: in a study of 437 college students, seven-site skinfolds read 1.4 points of body fat below DXA on average and a hand-held impedance device 4.9 points below (Burns et al., 2019). If you track body fat, use the same method each time. Our guide to how accurate each method is compares them, and BMI vs. body composition shows how the numbers relate.

Building and Keeping Lean Mass

For reaching a healthier weight in general, see our guide to lowering BMI.

Resistance training

Muscle-strengthening activity is how lean mass is built and kept. The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommend muscle-strengthening activities that work all major muscle groups on at least 2 days a week, alongside 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (HHS).

Sleep and recovery

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults aged 18 to 60 sleep 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis (Watson et al., 2015).

Frequently Asked Questions

Lean body mass (LBM) is your total body weight minus all fat weight. It includes muscles, bones, organs, skin, blood, and water: everything that isn't stored body fat. For example, a 180-pound man with 20% body fat has an LBM of 144 pounds and 36 pounds of fat.

The Boer formula is a population-based estimate: it predicts lean body mass from height, weight and sex only, so two people who match on all three get the same result whatever their build. A DXA scan measures lean mass instead; CDC calls DXA the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition.

Measured, it tells you more; estimated from height and weight, it doesn’t. BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass (CDC), and a scan that measures lean mass does. A formula estimate, this calculator’s included, starts from the same height and weight as BMI, so it can’t separate a muscular person from one with more fat at the same size. As a screening measure, BMI is quick, low-cost, and reliable in CDC’s words; our standard BMI calculator works it out.

No, lean body mass and muscle mass are related but not identical. LBM includes everything in your body that is not fat: skeletal muscle, smooth muscle (in organs), cardiac muscle, bones, connective tissue, organs, blood, skin, and water. When people talk about "gaining lean mass," they usually mean muscle, but LBM also rises with more bone or body water. The Boer formula estimates total LBM only and can't split it into muscle, bone and the rest.

Lean body mass naturally declines with age in a process called sarcopenia. Starting around age 30, adults lose an estimated 3–8% of their muscle mass per decade, and the rate is higher after age 60 (Volpi et al., 2004). Muscle-strengthening activity is part of the Physical Activity Guidelines at every adult age, including for older adults. Our BMI by age calculator explains how BMI is read at older ages.
