Key Takeaways
  • All adults (WHO): healthy BMI 18.5–24.9. WHO does not publish age-adjusted cut-offs.
  • Adults 65+ (Winter et al., Am J Clin Nutr, 2014 meta-analysis, n=197,940): lowest all-cause mortality observed across BMI 23–33, elevated below BMI 23. This is an observational association, not a treatment target.
  • Body composition (muscle mass, bone density, fat distribution) changes with age even when BMI does not — waist circumference and grip strength add information BMI cannot.

Medical Disclaimer: This calculator provides estimates for informational purposes only. The adult BMI cut-offs are the same at every age; the findings for adults 65+ below are observational and may not apply to every individual. Always consult a healthcare professional for medical advice.

Standard BMI range for adults

The standard BMI categories define 18.5–24.9 as the healthy range for all adults, and the CDC Adult BMI Categories does not carve out age-specific cut-offs. What does shift with age is body composition — muscle mass, bone density, and fat distribution — and, in observational data, the BMI band associated with the lowest all-cause mortality in older adults. The row for 65+ below summarises that observational finding; it is not a WHO recommendation.

Age Group BMI Range Basis
18–64 years 18.5 – 24.9 Same cut-offs at every adult age. Body composition shifts with age (see below).
65+ years 18.5 – 24.9 (WHO) Same cut-offs. Lowest mortality observed across BMI 23–33 in adults 65+ (Winter et al., 2014); observational, not a target.

WHO cut-offs: WHO obesity fact sheet. Older-adult observational data: Winter JE et al., 2014. Related epidemiology: Flegal KM et al., JAMA 2013.

What changes with age, and what doesn’t

The cut-offs don’t change. The adult categories (healthy weight 18.5 to under 25) apply at every age from 20 (CDC).

Body composition does. Muscle mass tends to fall by roughly 3–8% per decade after about age 30 (Volpi et al., 2004), so the same BMI can mean more fat later in life. In CDC’s body scans, average body fat rose from 25.4% in men in their 20s to 28.4% in their 50s, and from 36.9% to 40.3% in women (our calculation, NHANES 2011–2018; see the body fat percentage chart). Waist size, which in the tables above is larger in every age group from 30 on than at 20–29, is one way to see that change.

Height matters too. BMI divides by height squared, so if your height drops, your BMI rises at the same weight: going from 5′8″ to 5′6″ adds about 1.5 BMI points at a BMI of 25. Use your current measured height.

The Obesity Paradox Explained

The "obesity paradox" is an observational finding: in some large cohort studies, older adults classified as overweight (BMI 25–29.9) have shown similar or slightly lower all-cause mortality than those in the WHO normal range. It is an association in cohort data — not a clinical recommendation to gain weight. The current WHO healthy adult range (18.5–24.9) has not changed on the basis of these findings. See also the limitations of BMI.

What the meta-analysis actually reported

Flegal KM, Kit BK, Orpana H, Graubard BI. “Association of all-cause mortality with overweight and obesity using standard body mass index categories.” JAMA 2013;309(1):71–82 (PMID 23280227) pooled 97 studies covering ~2.88 million individuals and more than 270,000 deaths across all adult ages, and reported (relative to normal weight):

  • Overweight (BMI 25–29.9): hazard ratio 0.94 (95% CI 0.91–0.96) — ~6% lower all-cause mortality
  • Grade 1 obesity (BMI 30–34.9): hazard ratio 0.95 (95% CI 0.88–1.01) — not statistically distinguishable from normal weight
  • Grade 2+ obesity (BMI ≥35): hazard ratio 1.29 (95% CI 1.18–1.41) — ~29% higher mortality

What the research on older adults shows

In a meta-analysis of 32 cohort studies covering 197,940 adults aged 65 and older, all-cause mortality was higher below BMI 23 and did not rise again until BMI passed about 33 (Winter et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2014).

That is an observational finding, not a recommendation. Studies like this cannot show cause and effect; for example, people who have lost weight because of illness can make a lower BMI look riskier than it is. The CDC and WHO adult cut-offs stay the same at every age, so talk with a clinician about what weight range makes sense for you.

Obesity by age

Obesity (BMI 30 or higher) was most common in middle age in 2021–2023: 35.5% at ages 20–39, 46.4% at 40–59 and 38.9% at 60 and older (NCHS Data Brief 508). More detail: US obesity statistics.

Official guidance on activity and sleep

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommend 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week plus muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days, and for older adults, activity that includes balance training. For sleep, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society recommend at least 7 hours a night for adults 18–60 (via CDC).

BMI and age-related health conditions

Obesity is a recognised risk factor for a range of conditions that become more prevalent later in life: type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, several cancers (including postmenopausal breast, colorectal, endometrial, and kidney), obstructive sleep apnea, osteoarthritis, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Being underweight in later life carries its own risks, including osteoporosis, sarcopenia, frailty, and elevated all-cause mortality. Waist circumference and grip strength add information that BMI alone cannot — central fat distribution and preserved muscle function are independent predictors of outcomes.

For the specific-condition detail (mechanisms, thresholds, and the primary studies behind each association), see the WHO obesity and overweight fact sheet and the CDC adult obesity facts page. Our own guide on BMI and health risks summarises the associations with links to each of these sources.
