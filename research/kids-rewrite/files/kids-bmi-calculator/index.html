How BMI works for children and teens

For ages 2 through 19, BMI is calculated exactly as for adults, but it is judged differently: a child’s BMI is compared with other children of the same age and sex on CDC growth charts, and the result is a percentile. Children’s bodies change as they grow, and boys and girls change differently, so the fixed adult cut-offs don’t work for them (CDC).

BMI-for-age weight categories, ages 2–19 (CDC)
CategoryBMI-for-age percentile
UnderweightBelow the 5th percentile
Healthy weight5th to less than the 85th percentile
Overweight85th to less than the 95th percentile
Obesity95th percentile or higher
Severe obesity120% of the 95th percentile or higher, or a BMI of 35 or higher

A percentile is a ranking, not a grade. The 50th percentile is simply the middle: half of children of that age and sex have a lower BMI. Any percentile from the 5th to just under the 85th is in the healthy-weight category. Source: CDC child and teen BMI categories.

BMI-for-age percentile chart for boys and girls

These are the BMI values where each category begins, at each birthday, calculated from the CDC LMS reference data the calculator uses (BMI at the 5th, 85th and 95th percentiles). For a 14-year-old girl, for example, a BMI between the 5th- and 85th-percentile values in her row is in the healthy-weight range. Between birthdays the cut-offs shift a little month by month, which the calculator accounts for.

How this calculator works

It converts your child’s height and weight to BMI, then places that BMI on the CDC BMI-for-age reference for their exact age in months and sex, using the LMS method and the CDC extended BMI-for-age data files. For children at or above the 95th percentile it also shows BMI as a percentage of the 95th percentile, which is how severe obesity is defined. It gives one snapshot; a pediatrician looks at how the percentile changes over time.

How common is childhood obesity in the US?

In CDC’s measured survey for August 2021–August 2023, 21.1% of children and teens aged 2–19 had obesity: 14.9% at ages 2–5, 22.1% at ages 6–11 and 22.9% at ages 12–19. Severe obesity affected 7.0%. In 1971–1974 the obesity figure was 5.2% (NCHS Health E-Stat 112). Trends, sex and age breakdowns: childhood obesity statistics.

What childhood obesity is linked to

CDC lists these as more likely in children with obesity: high blood pressure and high cholesterol; impaired glucose tolerance, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes; breathing problems such as asthma and sleep apnea; joint problems and musculoskeletal discomfort; fatty liver disease, gallstones and reflux; anxiety and depression; low self-esteem; and social problems such as bullying and stigma. Children with obesity are also more likely to have obesity as adults (CDC childhood obesity facts).

CDC describes childhood obesity as having many contributing factors, including eating and physical-activity patterns, sleep routines, screen time, some medicines, genetics, and the places where children live, learn and play.

Official guidance on activity and sleep

Physical activity. The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommend that children and adolescents aged 6–17 get at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity every day, including vigorous, muscle-strengthening and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days a week, and that children aged 3–5 be physically active throughout the day.

Sleep. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 10–13 hours per 24 hours (including naps) at ages 3–5, 9–12 hours at ages 6–12 and 8–10 hours at ages 13–18; the American Academy of Pediatrics endorsed these recommendations (Paruthi et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2016).

When to talk to your child’s doctor

A single result is a starting point. Talk with your child’s pediatrician if the result is in the underweight, overweight or obesity range, if the percentile has moved a lot since the last check, or if you are worried about your child’s eating, growth or mood. The doctor can look at the growth trend and the whole child, which a calculator cannot.
