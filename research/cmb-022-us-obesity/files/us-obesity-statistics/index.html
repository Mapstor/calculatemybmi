US Obesity Rate and Statistics

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

What percentage of Americans are obese? Search for it and you can find 37%, 40.3%, 42.4% and 75% on the same results page.

Each of them comes from a real study. They differ in whether people were weighed or asked, in the years and ages they cover and, since 2025, in how obesity is defined. CDC’s National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, which weighs and measures a national sample, puts it at 40.3% of adults for August 2021 to August 2023 (CDC).

Below: the measured rates for adults and children since 1960, by age, sex, race and education, what each of the other numbers counts, and the data to download.

Key takeaways

40.3% of US adults have obesity, a BMI of 30 or higher: about 99 million people. That is CDC’s measured figure for adults 20 and older, August 2021 to August 2023. Another 32.1% are overweight, so 72.4% have a BMI of 25 or more, and 9.4% have severe obesity, a BMI of 40 or higher.

  • Obesity has about tripled since the early 1960s, from 13.4% to 40.8% of adults aged 20 to 74 (age-adjusted), while the share who are overweight stayed between 30% and 35%.
  • No significant change in the measured data: CDC found the change from 41.9% (2017 to March 2020) to the latest 40.3% not statistically significant, and severe obesity rose from 7.7% to 9.7% (age-adjusted) over the decade. Gallup’s self-reported rate fell from 39.9% in 2022 to 37.0% in 2025.
  • Highest at ages 40 to 59 (46.4%). Women and men have similar rates (41.3% and 39.2%), but severe obesity is almost twice as common in women (12.1% against 6.7%).
  • 21.1% of children and teens aged 2 to 19 have obesity, about 15.8 million, four times the 5.2% of 1971 to 1974.
  • The 68.6% and 75.2% figures use a new definition that adds waist measures to BMI. In the same people, BMI alone gave 42.9% and 39.9%.

How many Americans are obese?

About 99 million US adults had obesity in August 2021 to August 2023: 40.3% of the 245.1 million adults aged 20 and older that CDC’s survey represents. About 79 million more were overweight, and about 23 million had severe obesity.

Only about 1 in 4 adults (26.0%) has a BMI in the healthy range, fewer than have obesity. The typical adult is overweight: the median BMI is 28.2 for men and 28.3 for women, and the averages are 29.4 and 30.0 (CDC).

What percentage of Americans are overweight?

32.1% of US adults are overweight, a BMI of 25.0 to 29.9. Add the 40.3% with obesity and 72.4% have a BMI of 25 or higher, the figure behind claims that about 70% of Americans are overweight. Men are more often overweight than women, 35.5% against 28.1% (age-adjusted).

Unlike obesity, the overweight share ended where it started: 31.5% of adults aged 20 to 74 in 1960–1962 and 31.3% in 2021–2023 (age-adjusted), between 30% and 35% in every survey. The healthy-weight share shrank instead, from 51.1% to 26.2% (our calculation from CDC’s figures).

US obesity rate by year, 1960–2023

Adult obesity has about tripled since the early 1960s: 13.4% of adults aged 20 to 74 had obesity in 1960–1962 and 40.8% in 2021–2023 (age-adjusted). Severe obesity rose from 0.9% to 10.2%, 11 times as high.

The fastest climb came between the late 1970s and 2000, when the rate doubled from 15.0% (1976–1980) to 30.9% (1999–2000). It kept rising more slowly, to 42.8% in 2017–2018; the 40.8% of 2021–2023 is not significantly lower.

Are obesity rates rising or falling?

CDC’s measured data show no significant change. The latest CDC estimate, 40.3% of adults 20 and older (age-adjusted), is below the 41.9% of 2017 to March 2020, but CDC found neither that change nor the change over the decade since 2013–2014 (37.7%) statistically significant. Severe obesity did rise, from 7.7% to 9.7% (age-adjusted), and adult obesity remains above the Healthy People 2030 goal of 36.0% (CDC).

A survey where people give their own height and weight points down: the Gallup National Health and Well-Being Index put obesity at 39.9% in 2022 and 37.0% in 2025, with women at 38.8% and men at 35.2% (Gallup). CDC’s state surveys, also self-reported, show no clear change: 21 states were at 35% or higher in 2025 against 19 in 2024, and none reached 40% in 2025, but Mississippi (40.4% in 2024) had no 2025 estimate (CDC). Under the new definition that adds waist measures, one study found no change between 2017–2020 and 2021–2023 (Al-Roub et al., 2025).

Why do sources say 37%, 40%, 42% or 75%?

Each figure is right for what it counts. Three things move it: whether people were weighed or asked, which years and ages it covers, and which definition of obesity it uses.

Weighed or asked

In CDC’s survey, trained technicians measure height and weight in a mobile examination center. Surveys that ask people come in lower: Gallup says a “vanity effect” in how people describe themselves may explain why its estimates usually run somewhat below measured ones. CDC’s state figures are self-reported too, and every one of them in 2025, from 24.2% (the District of Columbia) to 39.7% (Alabama), sits below the national 40.3%: compare states with states, on the obesity rate by state page.

Which years and ages

42.4% (2017–2018) and 41.9% (2017 to March 2020) are CDC’s earlier figures, and neither is current. CDC’s adult figures start at age 20; Gallup and the state surveys include adults of 18 and 19. The 60-year series covers ages 20 to 74, which is why its latest value is 40.8%, not 40.3%.

Crude shares are the actual shares of adults: in 2021–2023, 40.3% with obesity, 32.1% overweight and 9.4% with severe obesity. Age-adjusted shares are reweighted to the age mix of the 2000 census so that years and groups compare fairly: 40.3%, 31.7% and 9.7%.

A new definition: BMI plus waist measures

In January 2025 the Lancet Commission on Clinical Obesity (58 experts, endorsed by 76 organizations) recommended confirming excess body fat instead of diagnosing obesity from BMI alone: a high BMI plus at least one waist measure above its cut-off (waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio or waist-to-height ratio), at least two waist measures whatever the BMI, or a direct body fat measurement. Above a BMI of 40, it says excess fat can be assumed. It asks for validated cut-offs appropriate to age, sex and ethnicity (Rubino et al., 2025).

Applied to 301,026 volunteers aged 18 to 80 in NIH’s All of Us program, a cohort not weighted to the US population, the new definition gave 68.6% against 42.9% by BMI with WHO’s race-specific cut-offs (Fourman et al., 2025). Applied to CDC’s 2017–2023 survey, with a waist more than half the height counted as one criterion, it gave 75.2% against 39.9%; 80.0% of adults had a waist more than half their height, and with 0.6 as the cut-off instead of 0.5 the study found 58.4% (Al-Roub et al., 2025). The Commission also splits obesity into clinical obesity, with signs of reduced organ function or a reduced ability to carry out daily activities, and preclinical obesity; in All of Us, 36.1% had clinical obesity. CDC’s figures still use BMI, and the waist-to-height ratio guide explains that measure.

Obesity rate by age, gender and education

By age: highest in middle age

Adults aged 40 to 59 have the highest rate, 46.4%, above both ages 20 to 39 (35.5%) and 60 and older (38.9%). The same middle-age peak shows in men and in women (CDC).

Men and women

Women and men have similar obesity rates, 41.3% and 39.2%, a difference CDC found not statistically significant. The gap is at the heavy end: 12.1% of women have severe obesity against 6.7% of men. Men are more often overweight (35.5% against 28.1%, age-adjusted).

By education

Adults with a bachelor’s degree or more have a lower rate (31.6%) than those with some college (45.0%) or a high school diploma or less (44.6%), and the last two don’t differ significantly. CDC found no significant differences between men and women at any education level.

Obesity rate by race and ethnicity

Age-adjusted obesity rates in 2021–2023 ranged from 13.1% among non-Hispanic Asian adults to 52.1% among non-Hispanic Black adults. They were 48.0% for Mexican American adults, 44.0% for Hispanic adults overall and 39.3% for non-Hispanic White adults. Among women, 56.0% of non-Hispanic Black women had obesity against 40.4% of non-Hispanic White women.

CDC adds two cautions. At a given BMI, body fat may vary by sex, age, and race and Hispanic origin; CDC points to research that health risks may begin at a lower BMI among Asian people, and that BMI overestimates body fat among Black people compared with others at the same BMI. And the 2021–2023 survey used a new sample design with no oversampling by race and Hispanic origin; two estimates for women did not meet NCHS standards of reliability. CDC prints them flagged, and the table below leaves them out.

What percentage of Americans are morbidly obese?

9.4% of US adults have severe obesity, CDC’s term for a BMI of 40 or higher, also called class 3 obesity: about 23 million adults. It is almost twice as common in women (12.1%) as in men (6.7%), and women’s rate is higher in every age group. It is more common at ages 20 to 39 (9.5%) and 40 to 59 (12.0%) than at 60 and older (6.6%). Over 60 years it rose from 0.9% to 10.2% of adults aged 20 to 74 (age-adjusted).

Childhood obesity rate in America

21.1% of US children and teens aged 2 to 19 had obesity in August 2021 to August 2023, including 7.0% with severe obesity, and another 15.1% were overweight: about 15.8 million children and teens with obesity (our calculation).

Obesity is lower at ages 2 to 5 (14.9%) than at 6 to 11 (22.1%) and 12 to 19 (22.9%). It was 23.0% among boys and 19.1% among girls. In 1971–1974, 5.2% of children and teens had obesity, about a quarter of the rate now.

Children are classified differently from adults: obesity means a BMI at or above the 95th percentile for their age and sex on CDC’s 2000 growth charts, and severe obesity 120% of that value or more. Method. Every rate comes from CDC’s examination surveys, the National Health Examination Survey for 1960–1962 and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) since 1971–1974, unless another source is named. Trained technicians measure height and weight in a mobile examination center, the results are weighted to represent the US civilian noninstitutionalized population, and pregnant women are excluded. The 2021–2023 adult figures rest on 5,929 examined adults aged 20 and older; the unweighted examination response rate, all ages, was 25.6%. Crude shares are the actual shares; age-adjusted shares are standardized to the 2000 census (ages 20 to 39, 40 to 59, and 60 and older; 60 to 74 for the 20-to-74 series). We call a difference higher or lower only where CDC reports it as statistically significant, or where it is more than 2.13 times its standard error (the 95% level for the survey’s 15 degrees of freedom), worked out from CDC’s published standard errors. Counts are a share times CDC’s population total for the survey (245.1 million adults, 74.9 million children aged 2 to 19), rounded. Our calculation from CDC’s 2021–2023 files (DEMO_L and BMX_L, weighted with the exam weights): the split of obesity into classes 1 and 2, and the mean BMI of all adults, 29.7; the same code reproduces CDC’s published 1.6%, 32.1%, 40.3% and 9.4% exactly. The share at 300 lb or more counts the 5,942 examined adults with a measured weight (CDC’s body measures file).
