How to Calculate Body Fat Percentage

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

How do you calculate your body fat percentage at home? How accurate is a body fat scale? Which method comes closest to a scan?

At home, you take a few measurements with a tape measure or skinfold calipers and put them into a published equation. A DXA scan, which CDC calls the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition (CDC), is the reference in the studies below. In a study that tested seven methods on the same 437 college students, all seven read lower than the scan on average, by 1.0 to 4.9 points (Burns et al., 2019).

Below: the steps for each home method, worked through for one example, how close the tested methods get to a scan, how body fat scales work, and how much a slip of the tape changes the answer.

Key takeaways

To calculate your body fat percentage at home, take a few body measurements and put them into a published equation. The simplest needs a tape measure and your height: men 64 − 20 × height ÷ waist, women 76 − 20 × height ÷ waist, with the waist measured at the top of the hip bones (relative fat mass). On CDC’s scans of US adults aged 20 to 59, it came within 5 points of the scan for 86% of men and 74% of women (our calculation).

  • Seven-site skinfolds had the smallest average miss of seven methods tested on the same college students, 11.7% of the scan’s reading, and a hand-held device the largest, 21.9% (Burns et al., 2019).
  • Three smart scales checked against DXA read fat mass 2.2 to 4.4 kg low, while their median weight differences from the scan were 0 to 0.3 kg (Frija-Masson et al., 2021).
  • A small measuring slip moves the answer by about a point: for the typical US adult aged 20 to 59, 1 inch on the waist moves the waist-and-height estimate by 0.9 points, and 5 lb on the scale moves the height-and-weight estimate by 0.9 to 1.0 (our calculation).
  • Methods disagree for the same person: in CDC’s survey of US adults aged 20 to 59, the two home formulas were more than 5 points apart for 38% of women and 15% of men (our calculation), so track one method, with the same device and the same measuring spots.
  • Before any test, copy the seven-method study’s routine: no large meal for 2 hours, no vigorous exercise for 12, no alcohol for 48, and an empty bladder.

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for information only. A body fat estimate is not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

How to calculate body fat percentage at home

Each home method is a published equation fed with a few measurements. The worked examples are for one man: 40 years old, 5′10″ (70 inches) and 180 lb, with a 15.5-inch neck, and a waist of 36 inches at the navel and 37 inches at the top of the hip bones. The body fat calculator does the arithmetic for all four methods.

Waist and height (relative fat mass)

  1. Measure your height without shoes.
  2. Measure your waist at the top of your hip bones (Cedars-Sinai). CDC’s survey measures the waist at the same level, the top of the right hip bone (Woolcott and Bergman, 2018).
  3. With both in the same unit, work out 64 − 20 × height ÷ waist for men, or 76 − 20 × height ÷ waist for women.

For the example man: 64 − 20 × 70 ÷ 37 = 26.2%. The formula uses the same two measurements as the waist-to-height ratio.

Tape measure (the Navy method)

  1. Measure your neck just below the Adam’s apple.
  2. Men: measure your waist at the navel. Women: measure your waist at its narrowest and your hips where the buttocks stick out most.
  3. Take each measurement three times and average them, as the Army’s 2013 worksheets do (DA Forms 5500 and 5501).
  4. Put the inches into the Navy equations: men 86.010 × log10(waist − neck) − 70.041 × log10(height) + 36.76; women 163.205 × log10(waist + hips − neck) − 97.684 × log10(height) − 78.387 (US Army Table B-5).

For the example man: 86.010 × log10(36 − 15.5) − 70.041 × log10(70) + 36.76 = 20.4%.

Height and weight (the BMI formula)

  1. Work out your BMI: weight in pounds × 703 ÷ height in inches squared, rounded to one decimal (the BMI calculator does this).
  2. Body fat % = 1.20 × BMI + 0.23 × age − 10.8 × sex − 5.4, with sex 1 for men and 0 for women (Deurenberg et al., 1991).

For the example man: BMI = 180 × 703 ÷ 70² = 25.8, then 1.20 × 25.8 + 0.23 × 40 − 10.8 − 5.4 = 24.0%.

Skinfold calipers (3 sites)

  1. Measure a skinfold with the calipers, in millimeters, at three sites: chest, abdomen and thigh for men; triceps, suprailiac (just above the hip bone) and thigh for women.
  2. Measure each site twice and take a third reading if the two differ by more than 2 mm, as the trained measurers in the seven-method study did (Burns et al., 2019).
  3. Add the three, turn the sum into body density with the Jackson–Pollock equation, then into body fat with Siri’s: 495 ÷ density − 450.

For the example man, skinfolds of 15, 25 and 18 mm add up to 58: density = 1.10938 − 0.0008267 × 58 + 0.0000016 × 58² − 0.0002574 × 40 = 1.0565, and 495 ÷ 1.0565 − 450 = 18.5%. For women the density equation is 1.0994921 − 0.0009929 × S + 0.0000023 × S² − 0.0001392 × age, where S is the sum. The seven-method study measured seven sites, with trained measurers; no study cited here checked the 3-site version against DXA.

Four methods, four answers for one man: 18.5% from calipers, 20.4% from the tape, 24.0% from height and weight and 26.2% from waist and height.

How close tested methods get to a DXA scan

Studies that test one method each use different people and different scanners, so the clearest comparison puts every method on the same people. Burns and colleagues did that for seven methods in 437 college students, mean age 19, against one DXA scanner (Hologic Discovery W). Every method read lower than the scan on average, and for all but the four-electrode analyzer the gap grew as body fat rose (Burns et al., 2019).

Below DXA: how far below the scan the method read on average (the 90% confidence intervals are in the figure at the top). Average miss: the average distance from the scan as a share of the scan’s reading (mean absolute percentage error), so at 30% body fat a 10% miss is 3 points. Average difference inside the study’s ±2.7-point equivalence margin (10% of the 27.0% average scan): underwater weighing, skinfolds, the Bod Pod and the four-electrode analyzer; single readings missed by more.

The two home formulas on CDC’s scans. On CDC’s scans of US adults aged 20 to 59, the waist-and-height formula missed by 3.1 points on average and the height-and-weight formula by 4.4 points: 10.4% and 14.0% of the scan’s reading (our calculation). Those shares don’t line up with the college study’s. The people and scanners differ, and CDC’s scans read 32.9% body fat on average against the students’ 27.0%, so the same miss in points is a smaller share of a higher reading.

Three more comparisons with DXA

  • Tape measure: in 1,407 US Army recruits, the Navy method read 6.0 points below DXA on average, with a standard deviation of 4.4 points for women and 3.5 for men (Foulis et al., 2023).
  • InBody 770: in 226 adults aged 18 to 80, it read 3.1 points below DXA on average, and for about 95% of them it landed between 2.6 points above and 8.9 points below the scan; its underestimate was largest at normal weight and shrank as BMI rose. A SECA mBCA 515 in the same people read 0.3 points below on average (Lahav et al., 2021).
  • Smart scales: three home scales checked against DXA in 48 to 53 hospital patients each read fat mass low, by a median of 2.2 to 4.4 kg (Frija-Masson et al., 2021).

How accurate are body fat scales?

A body fat scale runs an impedance test: it passes a small current between electrodes under your feet and measures the impedance, the body’s opposition to the current. CDC’s documentation for its own impedance test, which used electrodes on the right hand and foot, explains the rest: an equation turns the impedance into fat-free mass, fat mass is your weight minus that, and percent fat, worked out from those estimates, is a third-order estimate, “subject to additional error” (CDC).

In the seven-method study, the foot-to-foot scale (Tanita BF-556) read 3.5 points below DXA on average, with an average miss of 17.2%, and the hand-held device (Omron HBF-306) read 4.9 points below, with 21.9%, the largest miss of the seven. The lab analyzer, with four electrodes on the right hand and foot while lying down, read 1.6 points below (Burns et al., 2019).

Three smart scales sold for home use (Tefal Body Partner, Terraillon DietPack and Withings Body Cardio) were checked against DXA in patients referred for a scan at a Paris hospital. Their median weight differences from the scan’s body mass were 0.3, 0 and 0.25 kg, but each underestimated fat mass, by a median of 2.2, 4.4 and 3.7 kg (Frija-Masson et al., 2021).

Water moves the reading: dehydration can push the body fat estimate up and overhydration can push it down (Burns et al., 2019). To follow a trend, weigh in under the same conditions every time, with the routine in the next section. CDC’s survey did not run its impedance test on pregnant women, or on people with a pacemaker or automatic defibrillator or with artificial joints, pins or plates (CDC).

Repeat and average. The Army’s worksheets take each tape measurement three times and average them to the nearest half inch; the seven-method study measured each skinfold twice and took a third reading when the two differed by more than 2 mm.

Before any test. The seven-method study asked for a routine you can copy for any method: no large meal in the 2 hours before, no vigorous exercise in the 12 hours before, no alcohol in the 48 hours before, no more than two glasses of water, finished at least 2 hours before, and an empty bladder (Burns et al., 2019).

Compare like with like. Two methods can differ by several points for the same person: in CDC’s survey of US adults aged 20 to 59, the waist-and-height and height-and-weight formulas were more than 5 points apart for 38% of women and 15% of men, and the median gap was 4.1 points for women and 2.4 for men (our calculation). Track one method, one device and the same measuring spots.

Lab and gym methods: DXA, Bod Pod and underwater weighing

DXA scan

A whole-body X-ray scan that measures bone and soft tissue and splits the soft tissue into fat and lean. CDC uses it in its national survey and calls it the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition, in part for its speed, ease of use and low radiation, less than 20 µSv for a whole-body scan (CDC). The software matters too: CDC’s scans were analyzed with an option that adds 5% of lean mass to the fat mass, and the scanner table kept out people over 450 lb or taller than 6′5″ (CDC).

Underwater weighing

You breathe out fully and are weighed under water; with your weight on land, that gives your body density, which an equation turns into body fat. In the seven-method study it read 1.0 point below DXA on average, the closest of the seven, with an average miss of 13.4% (Burns et al., 2019).

Bod Pod (air displacement)

You sit in a sealed chamber that measures the air you displace, which gives your body volume and so your density. It read 1.6 points below DXA on average, with an average miss of 14.5% (Burns et al., 2019).

Near-infrared

A light wand held on the biceps (the study used a Futrex-6100). It read 4.2 points below DXA on average, with an average miss of 18.7%, and was one of the two methods that failed even the study’s looser 15% equivalence test (Burns et al., 2019).

What your number means

Provisional ranges tied to BMI call 21 to 32.9% body fat healthy for women aged 20 to 39 and 8 to 19.9% for men, with higher ranges at older ages (Gallagher et al., 2000). On CDC’s scans of US adults aged 20 to 39, the median was about 38% for women and 26% for men. Method. Adults aged 20 to 59 with a valid whole-body DXA scan in NHANES 2011–2012 to 2017–2018, pregnant women excluded, weighted with CDC’s exam weights divided by four: the sample of the body fat calculator’s check. For each person we worked out both formulas from their measurements and each formula’s distance from the scan, in points and as a share of the scan’s reading (averaged, the average miss); we also worked out how far the two formulas landed from each other, and how much a 1-inch or 5-lb slip moves each formula with the person’s own height, waist and weight. Averages and shares are survey-weighted; each share quoted here has a 95% margin of error under 2 percentage points and each average miss under 0.4 (Taylor linearization over the survey’s 59 strata and 121 primary sampling units); medians use the weighted midpoint rule. Every figure is rounded once, from the unrounded value. The worked example and the tape and caliper slips are arithmetic on the published equations.
