Key takeaways

BMI for men uses the same formula and cut-offs as for women: weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared, or pounds × 703 ÷ inches². 18.5 to 24.9 is the healthy range, 25 to 29.9 is overweight and 30 or more is obesity. NIH guidelines add a waist cut-off for men: more than 40 inches (102 cm).

  • The average US man has a BMI of 29.4, and about 3 in 4 are at 25 or above (CDC, 2021–2023).
  • Muscle raises BMI without adding fat, so a muscular man can score overweight.
  • Waist size adds what BMI misses: about 3 in 10 US men with an overweight BMI have a waist over 40 inches.
  • The cut-offs are the same at every adult age.

Medical disclaimer: This calculator gives estimates for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

How to read your result

Every number below uses the calculator’s imperial starting values: a man who is 5′10″ (177.8 cm) and weighs 180 lb (81.6 kg).

BMI: 25.8, overweight
Weight divided by height squared, rounded to one decimal; the category comes from that rounded number. CDC cut-offs for adults: under 18.5 underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 healthy, 25 to 29.9 overweight, 30 and above obesity.
Healthy weight: 129–173 lb
The lightest and heaviest whole-pound weights at your height with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9; in whole kilograms, 59–78 kg. It depends only on height. In imperial mode the calculator also shows each limit converted (58.5 and 78.5 kg).
7 lb (3.2 kg) above the healthy range
The distance from your weight to the nearest end of that range, as imperial mode shows it (3.2 kg is 7 lb converted).
Compared with US men: higher than 31%
Where your BMI falls among men aged 20 and over whom CDC measured in 2021–2023 (next section).
BMI Prime: 1.03
Your BMI divided by 25, where the overweight range starts. Under 1.00 is below it; 1.03 is 3% over.
Ponderal index: 14.5 kg/m³
Weight divided by height cubed. There are no official adult cut-offs, so compare it only with your own earlier results.
Weight milestones
The weight where each CDC range starts at your height. At 5′10″: healthy from 129 lb, overweight from 174 lb, obesity from 209 lb, class 2 obesity from 244 lb and class 3 from 279 lb.
What-if table
Your BMI and category after gaining or losing 5, 10, 15 or 20 lb (2.5 to 10 kg in metric).

When BMI misleads men

BMI can’t tell muscle from fat. In CDC body scans of US men aged 20 to 59, body fat varied widely within every BMI category, and the categories overlap:

One in ten men with an overweight BMI had less body fat (under 21.7%) than the typical man with a healthy BMI (22.1%).

Muscular men and athletes

Very muscular men can land in the overweight or obesity range with little body fat. At the NFL Scouting Combine, 53.4% of prospects had a BMI of 30 or more, but only 8.9% had obesity by measured body fat (Provencher et al., 2018). If you lift or play a power sport, estimate your body fat with the body fat calculator; BMI for athletes goes further.

What to check alongside BMI

  • Waist size: more than 40 in (102 cm) is the NIH cut-off for men (next section).
  • Body fat percentage: DXA is the reference method and underwater weighing came closest to it; at home, skinfold calipers come closest (how accurate each method is).
  • Waist-to-height ratio: your waist divided by your height; the waist-to-height ratio guide covers the 0.5 boundary and its sources.
  • Blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol: routine checks that BMI can’t replace.

Waist size for men: the 40-inch cut-off

NIH guidelines treat a waist of more than 40 inches (102 cm) as a marker of higher risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease in men. The cut-off matters most at a BMI of 25 to 34.9; at 35 and above nearly every man is over it (NHLBI).

The waist adds the most in the overweight range. About 3 in 10 US men with a BMI of 25 to 29.9 have a waist over 102 cm; the other 7 don’t, though their BMI category is the same.

Waist-to-hip ratio

Divide your waist by your hip measurement. A WHO expert consultation (2008) uses a ratio of 0.90 or more in men as the cut-off for a substantially increased risk of metabolic complications.

How to measure your waist

  1. Stand up straight and breathe out normally. Don’t pull your stomach in.
  2. Put a tape measure around your bare waist, just above your hip bones.
  3. Keep the tape snug and level all the way round, without pressing into the skin.
  4. Read it at the end of a normal breath out, to the nearest half inch (or 1 cm).

Measure at the same time of day so the numbers compare. To log waist and BMI together, see the BMI tracking guide.

BMI and health risks for men

NIH guidelines link a BMI above the healthy range with higher risk of high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, sleep apnea and osteoarthritis (NHLBI).

In a large trial of adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, losing 5 to 10% of body weight was linked to improvements in cardiovascular risk factors within a year (Wing et al., 2011).

A BMI under 18.5 can reflect undernutrition or an illness and is also worth checking with a doctor. The evidence behind each risk is in BMI and health risks.

When to see a doctor

  • Your BMI is 30 or higher, or under 18.5.
  • Your waist is more than 40 inches (102 cm).
  • Your weight changes quickly without a clear reason.
  • You snore loudly, stop breathing or gasp in your sleep, or feel very sleepy during the day.
  • Heart disease, type 2 diabetes or stroke runs in your family.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the cut-offs used by the calculator and the tables.
  • Fryar CD, Afful J, Saif NT. NCHS Health E-Stat 111 (Feb 2026): overweight, obesity and severe obesity in US men, August 2021–August 2023, age-adjusted.
  • CDC NCHS. Vital and Health Statistics Series 3, No. 50: average and median BMI of US men.
  • CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 exam files (DEMO_L, BMX_L): our calculation of averages by age, BMI percentiles and waist shares for men aged 20 and over (2,680 with BMI, 2,605 with waist), weighted with the exam weights.
  • CDC NHANES 2011–2018 DXA body scans: our analysis of body fat by BMI category, men aged 20 to 59 (body fat percentage chart).
  • NHLBI. Clinical Guidelines on the Identification, Evaluation, and Treatment of Overweight and Obesity in Adults (NIH, 1998) and Overweight and Obesity: waist cut-off, conditions linked to excess weight, measuring method.
  • WHO. Waist circumference and waist–hip ratio: report of a WHO expert consultation, Geneva, 2008.
  • Provencher MT, et al. Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2018: BMI and body fat at the NFL Scouting Combine.
  • Wing RR, et al. Diabetes Care, 2011: weight loss and cardiovascular risk factors after one year (Look AHEAD).
  • Winter JE, et al. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2014: BMI and mortality in adults 65 and over.
  • Gallagher D, et al. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2000: provisional body fat ranges matched to BMI cut-offs, by age.

Method. BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)², or lb × 703 ÷ in², rounded to one decimal; the category uses the rounded value. Healthy limits are the lightest and heaviest whole pounds (or kilograms) whose rounded BMI is 18.5 to 24.9. Percentiles come from the survey-weighted distribution of measured BMI with linear interpolation between values; the same method reproduces NCHS’s published medians (BMI 28.2 men, 28.3 women) and 95th-percentile weights (290.6 lb men, 262.7 lb women). Data: CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 and 2011–2018.
