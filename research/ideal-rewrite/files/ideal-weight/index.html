Key Takeaways
  • Four clinical formulas -- Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi provide different estimates
  • Body frame matters -- small, medium, and large frames have different ideal weight ranges
  • "Ideal weight" is a range, not a number -- there's no single perfect weight (see healthy BMI range)
  • These formulas have limitations -- they were developed from specific populations and may not apply to everyone (learn about BMI categories)
  • Use alongside other metrics -- combine with BMI, lean body mass, and waist-to-height ratio for a fuller picture

Medical Disclaimer: These formulas provide estimates based on height and sex. "Ideal weight" is a clinical concept, not a universal standard. Individual health depends on many factors beyond weight. The Four Ideal Weight Formulas Explained

Our calculator uses four well-established clinical formulas. Each was developed in a different decade, by different researchers, for slightly different purposes. Understanding where they come from helps you interpret the results. For a broader look at how weight-related formulas work, see our BMI formula guide.

1. Devine Formula (1974)

Dr. B.J. Devine published these equations in a 1974 article on dosing the antibiotic gentamicin. They became the most cited ideal body weight equations: by 2000, more than 200 articles had cited them (Pai & Paloucek, 2000).

Men: 50 + 2.3 x (height in inches - 60) kg
Women: 45.5 + 2.3 x (height in inches - 60) kg

The formula uses 60 inches (5'0") as the baseline, adding 2.3 kg for every inch of height above that baseline. Note that this means it cannot calculate ideal weight for people shorter than 5'0".

2. Robinson Formula (1983)

Dr. J.D. Robinson and colleagues published this equation in 1983, derived by regression analysis of height–weight data. It came out strikingly similar to Devine’s (Pai & Paloucek, 2000). It uses a lower per-inch increment, especially for women.

Men: 52 + 1.9 x (height in inches - 60) kg
Women: 49 + 1.7 x (height in inches - 60) kg

The Robinson formula starts men at a slightly higher baseline (52 kg vs 50 kg) but increases more slowly per inch (1.9 vs 2.3). For women, the baseline is notably higher (49 kg vs 45.5 kg) but the per-inch increase is much lower (1.7 vs 2.3).

3. Miller Formula (1983)

Also published in 1983, Dr. D.R. Miller proposed this formula with a higher baseline weight and the lowest per-inch increment of all four formulas. This means the Miller formula tends to give the highest ideal weight for shorter individuals but converges with the others at greater heights.

Men: 56.2 + 1.41 x (height in inches - 60) kg
Women: 53.1 + 1.36 x (height in inches - 60) kg

With a baseline of 56.2 kg for men and 53.1 kg for women, the Miller equation gives the highest results at shorter heights; at taller heights the other equations overtake it. Miller and colleagues derived it from the 1983 Metropolitan Life height–weight tables (Pai & Paloucek, 2000).

4. Hamwi Formula (1964)

The oldest of the four formulas, developed by Dr. G.J. Hamwi. This formula was introduced as a quick rule-of-thumb for clinicians. In pounds it is 100 lb for women and 106 lb for men at 5 feet, plus 5 lb (women) or 6 lb (men) per inch above that.

Men: 48 + 2.7 x (height in inches - 60) kg
Women: 45.5 + 2.2 x (height in inches - 60) kg

The Hamwi formula shares the same baseline as the Devine formula for women (45.5 kg) but has a slightly lower per-inch increment (2.2 vs 2.3). For men, Hamwi starts lower (48 kg) but uses the highest per-inch value (2.7), so it gives the highest results for tall men.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Woman, 5'6" (66 inches)

Height in inches = 66. We calculate (66 - 60) = 6 inches above the baseline.

  • Devine: 45.5 + 2.3 x 6 = 45.5 + 13.8 = 59.3 kg (130.7 lbs)
  • Robinson: 49 + 1.7 x 6 = 49 + 10.2 = 59.2 kg (130.5 lbs)
  • Miller: 53.1 + 1.36 x 6 = 53.1 + 8.16 = 61.3 kg (135.1 lbs)
  • Hamwi: 45.5 + 2.2 x 6 = 45.5 + 13.2 = 58.7 kg (129.4 lbs)

Average: 59.6 kg (131.4 lbs). The four formulas agree closely at this height, ranging from about 129 to 135 lbs. Compare this with the BMI calculator for women to see the healthy BMI range.

Example 2: Man, 5'10" (70 inches)

Height in inches = 70. We calculate (70 - 60) = 10 inches above the baseline.

  • Devine: 50 + 2.3 x 10 = 50 + 23 = 73.0 kg (160.9 lbs)
  • Robinson: 52 + 1.9 x 10 = 52 + 19 = 71.0 kg (156.5 lbs)
  • Miller: 56.2 + 1.41 x 10 = 56.2 + 14.1 = 70.3 kg (155.0 lbs)
  • Hamwi: 48 + 2.7 x 10 = 48 + 27 = 75.0 kg (165.3 lbs)

Average: 72.3 kg (159.4 lbs). At this height, the formulas show more variation -- from 155 to 165 lbs. The Hamwi formula gives the highest result, while Miller gives the lowest. You can cross-check this with the BMI calculator for men.

Method 2: Elbow Breadth

The 1983 Metropolitan Life tables defined frame size by elbow breadth: with the forearm bent upward at 90 degrees, the width across the elbow is compared with height-specific reference values (Pai & Paloucek, 2000).

How Frame Size Adjusts Ideal Weight

Once you know your frame size, the traditional adjustment from Hamwi's rule of thumb allows about 10% above or below the calculated weight (Pai & Paloucek, 2000):

Small Frame
Subtract 10% from medium-frame ideal weight
Medium Frame
Use the standard formula result as-is
Large Frame
Add 10% to medium-frame ideal weight

For example, if your medium-frame ideal weight is 150 lbs, a small frame person of the same height would target about 135 lbs, while a large frame person would target about 165 lbs. Learn more about how body composition interacts with weight metrics in our body fat vs BMI guide.

Ideal Weight vs. healthy Weight: Understanding the Difference

Many people use "ideal weight" and "healthy weight" interchangeably, but they represent different concepts. Understanding the distinction is crucial for setting realistic health goals. Our healthy BMI range guide explores this in more detail, and our BMI guide for women and BMI guide for men provide gender-specific context.

Ideal Weight (Formulas)

  • Gives a single target number
  • Based on height and sex only
  • Derived from statistical models
  • Originally designed for drug dosing and clinical shortcuts
  • Does not account for age, muscle mass, or ethnicity

Healthy Weight (BMI Range)

  • Gives a broad range (BMI 18.5 - 24.9)
  • Based on population health outcome data
  • Accounts for a wider variety of body types
  • Used to classify weight status by the WHO and CDC
  • Still does not measure body composition directly

Why the Range Matters More Than a Single Number

For a 5′8″ woman, the four equations average about 140 lbs. The healthy BMI range at that height runs from about 122 to 164 lbs, a 42-pound window. The single formula number sits inside that range, but it is one point in it, not the only healthy weight.

Neither approach accounts for lean body mass. An athlete with significant muscle could weigh well above their "ideal weight" while having excellent metabolic health. Conversely, a sedentary person at their exact "ideal weight" may still have concerning body fat levels. For a deeper exploration, read our article on muscle mass and BMI.

Bottom line: Use the ideal weight calculation as a general reference point, not a precise target. The healthy BMI range gives you more flexibility, and body composition measurements like lean body mass and waist-to-height ratio provide a more complete picture of your health.

History of Ideal Weight Formulas

The concept of an "ideal" body weight has evolved significantly over the past century. Understanding this history helps put the calculator results in context and reveals why no single formula should be treated as definitive truth. The broader history of weight assessment is covered in our BMI history guide.

Early Insurance Tables (1940s-1950s)

The idea of a “desirable” or “ideal” weight comes from insurance height–weight tables: weight data from these tables were found to correlate with mortality, which led to those terms (Pai & Paloucek, 2000).

The Hamwi Formula (1964)

Dr. G.J. Hamwi published a quick clinical rule of thumb in 1964, so clinicians could estimate a weight without looking up tables.

The Devine Formula (1974)

The Devine equations came from pharmacology: they appeared in a 1974 article on gentamicin dosing, and Pai and Paloucek found them consistent with an older rule of thumb developed from height–weight tables (Pai & Paloucek, 2000). They became the most cited ideal body weight equations.

Robinson and Miller (1983)

In 1983, Robinson et al. and Miller et al. independently derived equations from height–weight data. Both turned out strikingly similar to Devine’s, which Pai and Paloucek explain by the general agreement among the height–weight tables they all came from (Pai & Paloucek, 2000).

Modern Context

Today the equations are still used in clinical drug dosing. For judging a healthy weight, the BMI range is the standard (CDC). As reviewed by Peterson and colleagues, the equations don’t line up with BMI: compared with a fixed BMI, they give too little weight at shorter heights and too much at taller heights (Peterson et al., 2016).

Limitations and Criticisms of Ideal Weight Formulas

While these formulas remain in widespread use, they have significant limitations that users should understand. Many of these issues parallel the limitations of BMI itself. For a deeper understanding of body composition, see our lean body mass calculator guide and learn about body fat percentage vs BMI.

Population Bias

The equations come from US height–weight tables (Pai & Paloucek, 2000) and take no account of ethnicity, age or body composition.

No Age Adjustment

None of the four formulas account for age, yet body composition changes substantially throughout life. Older adults naturally lose muscle mass and bone density, while younger adults may carry more lean mass. Our BMI by age calculator explains how BMI is read at different ages.

Ignores Body Composition

The formulas treat all weight as equal, making no distinction between muscle, fat, bone, and water. A muscular athlete and a sedentary person of the same height and sex get the same "ideal weight." Use our lean body mass calculator for a composition-aware estimate. See also: BMI for athletes.

Height Limitations

All four equations start at 5 feet (60 inches) and add weight for each inch above that, so they were not written for shorter heights. Toward the extremes of height they also drift away from the BMI-based healthy range (Peterson et al., 2016).

No Validation Studies

Pai and Paloucek concluded that, because the equations come from similar height–weight tables, any one of them may be used to estimate ideal body weight (Pai & Paloucek, 2000). That makes them interchangeable estimates, not health targets.

Better Alternatives Exist

Modern health assessment increasingly relies on body composition analysis (DEXA scans, bioelectrical impedance), waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, and metabolic health markers. These add information about body composition and fat distribution that a height-based formula cannot.

The calculated ideal weight should be treated as a general reference point, not a strict target. A more useful goal is to fall within the healthy BMI range (18.5 - 24.9) while maintaining good metabolic health markers (blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol). Focus on overall health behaviors -- regular physical activity, balanced nutrition, adequate sleep, and stress management -- rather than hitting an exact number on the scale. If you are concerned about your weight, consult a healthcare professional who can evaluate your individual situation, including body composition, medical history, and health goals. If your current weight is significantly above or below the calculated ideal, don't panic. First, remember that these formulas have known limitations and may not apply to your body type. If you are muscular, the formulas will underestimate your healthy weight. Second, in a large trial of adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, losing 5–10% of body weight was linked to improvements in blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol within a year (Wing et al., 2011). Set incremental goals rather than fixating on the formula number. Work with a healthcare provider or registered dietitian to develop a safe, sustainable plan. The ideal weight formulas and BMI both use height and weight, but in different ways. BMI is calculated from your actual weight and tells you which category you fall into (underweight, normal, overweight, obese). Ideal weight formulas work in reverse -- they use only your height to predict what you "should" weigh. The ideal weight from these formulas typically corresponds to a BMI of roughly 21-23, which is near the middle of the "normal" range.
