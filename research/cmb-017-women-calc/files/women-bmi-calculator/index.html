Key takeaways

BMI for women uses the same formula and CDC cut-offs as for men: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, or pounds × 703 ÷ inches². 18.5 to 24.9 is a healthy weight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight and 30 or more is obesity, at every adult age.

  • The median US woman has a BMI of 28.3, and 28.1% of women have one in the healthy range (CDC measurements, 2021–2023).
  • At 5′4″ (162.6 cm), a healthy weight is 108 to 145 lb (49 to 65 kg).
  • Since June 2026 the waist counts too: 69.4% of US women meet the American Diabetes Association’s new obesity thresholds, against 41.8% of the same women by BMI alone (our calculation).
  • At the same BMI women carry more fat: on CDC’s scans of adults aged 20 to 59 with a healthy BMI, women had a median of 33.4% body fat and men 22.1%.
  • In pregnancy the categories don’t apply; CDC’s advice on weight gain uses your BMI before pregnancy.

Medical disclaimer: This calculator gives estimates for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

How to read your result

Every number below uses the calculator’s starting values, 5′4″ (162.6 cm) and 140 lb (63.5 kg), with a waist of 33 inches (83.8 cm) entered in the optional field.

BMI: 24.0, healthy weight
140 × 703 ÷ 64² = 24.0, rounded to one decimal (CDC formula); the category comes from that rounded number. CDC’s adult cut-offs: under 18.5 underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 healthy, 25 to 29.9 overweight, 30 and above obesity.
Healthy weight: 108–145 lb (49–65 kg)
The lightest and heaviest whole pounds at your height with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, and the same in whole kilograms. It depends only on height. The line under the tiles says how far you are from the range when you are outside it.
Compared with US women: higher than 24%
About 24% of women aged 20 and over, measured by CDC in 2021–2023, have a lower BMI than yours and about 75% a higher one, read off the percentiles in the next section. The text adds the figure for women aged 20 to 39 (32%), 40 to 59 (19%) and 60 and over (21%).
US women with a BMI like yours
On CDC’s body scans, women aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 24 to 24.9 had a median of 36.1% body fat (middle half 33.6% to 38.7%), and among US women with a healthy BMI, 50.2% have a waist at least half their height.
Waist check: ratio 0.51
33 ÷ 64 = 0.516, which the check shows as 0.51: it cuts the ratio to two decimals, so 0.50 always means a waist of at least half the height. That is increased central adiposity on NICE’s scale, and under the 35-inch line in US guidelines. Under the 2026 ADA standards, for women of non-Asian background the waist doesn’t change the class at a BMI under 25. For women of Asian background the line is 23, so for them a BMI of 24.0 with this waist counts as obesity.
BMI Prime: 0.96
Your BMI divided by 25, where overweight starts; under 1.00 is below it.
Ponderal index: 14.8 kg/m³
Weight in kilograms divided by height in meters cubed. CDC’s adult categories use BMI, not this index, so compare it only with your own earlier results.
Weight milestones
The weight where each range starts at your height. At 5′4″: healthy from 108 lb, overweight from 146 lb, obesity from 175 lb, class 2 obesity from 204 lb and class 3 from 233 lb.

BMI for women by age

CDC applies its adult categories “regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC), so 18.5 to 24.9 is a healthy BMI at 70 as at 20. US women’s average BMI differs by age group: 28.0 for women in their 20s and 31.4 in their 50s, then 29.5 at 70 to 79 and 27.7 at 80 and over (our calculation; each difference is larger than its margin of error). These are different women measured in the same years, not the same women followed as they aged. 40.6% of women in their 20s have a BMI in the healthy range, against 23.3% in their 50s.

Women 60 and over

Some senior BMI calculators online class a BMI of 23 to 29.9 (or 23 to 30) as normal for adults over 65 and list the meta-analysis below among their sources. That range isn’t CDC’s. In that meta-analysis of 197,940 people aged 65 and over, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014): an association, not a healthy range or a target.

Muscle is the other caveat: “Loss of muscle mass in older adults reduces the accuracy of BMI, which may lead to underdiagnosis of obesity” (ADA, 2026). US women aged 70 to 79 have a median BMI of 28.0, and 25.7% have one in the healthy range. The BMI calculator by age and BMI by age cover older adults in more depth.

Waist size and the 2026 ADA standards

BMI can’t show where fat sits; a tape measure can. Three rules use the waist, and the calculator’s waist check applies all three:

  • US guidelines treat a waist over 35 inches (88 cm) as a sign of higher risk for women, mainly at a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI).
  • NICE (UK) classes a waist-to-height ratio of 0.4 to 0.49 as healthy central adiposity, 0.5 to 0.59 as increased and 0.6 or more as high, for adults of both sexes and all ethnicities with a BMI under 35 (NICE).
  • The American Diabetes Association’s 2026 standards count obesity from a BMI of 30, or from 25 with a waist of at least half the height or 88 cm (34.6 in) in women. For women of Asian background the lines are a BMI of 27.5, or 23 with a waist of at least half the height or 80 cm (31.5 in). The standards cover adults who aren’t pregnant (ADA, 2026).

Among US women with a healthy BMI, 16.8% have a waist of 88 cm or more and 50.2% one at least half their height; with an overweight BMI, 79.1% and 95.5% (our calculation, 3,113 women with a measured waist). Applied to 3,216 women, the ADA thresholds class 69.4% as obesity, against 41.8% of the same women by BMI alone.

The waist-to-height ratio guide shows how to measure, and the BMI chart for women breaks the waist shares down further.

Body fat at the same BMI

BMI counts muscle, bone and fat alike, and at the same BMI women carry more fat than men. On CDC’s body scans of adults aged 20 to 59, women with a healthy BMI had a median of 33.4% body fat (middle half 29.8% to 36.5%), men 22.1% (18.6% to 25.0%). At an overweight BMI the women’s median was 39.5%, and with obesity 44.0% (our calculation from NHANES 2011–2018 DXA scans).

After you calculate, the line on US women with a BMI like yours gives the median for your one-point band of BMI (24 to 24.9, for example). For a reading of your own, the body fat calculator estimates it from a tape measure, and the body fat percentage chart shows where a reading sits among US women.

Asian and other backgrounds

CDC’s categories don’t change with race: they apply “regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC). Other bodies use lower lines for some groups:

  • CDC’s National Diabetes Prevention Program takes Asian American adults from a BMI of 23 instead of 25 (CDC).
  • A WHO expert consultation kept the international categories for everyone and named BMI 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 as potential public-health action points for Asian populations (WHO, 2004).
  • In the UK, NICE uses overweight from 23 to 27.4 and obesity from 27.5 for people of South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background (NICE guideline NG246, a 2022 recommendation).

The 2026 ADA standards also set lower lines for adults of Asian background: obesity from a BMI of 27.5, or from 23 with a waist at least half the height or 80 cm (31.5 in) in women. When your waist is entered, the waist check shows that result too whenever it differs.

BMI and pregnancy

The adult categories are for women who aren’t pregnant: NCHS leaves pregnant women out of its adult BMI statistics, as the US comparison on this page does. During pregnancy, CDC’s advice on weight gain depends on your BMI before pregnancy, using recommendations from the Institute of Medicine (CDC):

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 demographics and body measures files (DEMO_L, BMX_L): our calculation for 3,249 women aged 20 and over (BMI percentiles by age, BMI by decade), 3,113 with a measured waist and 3,216 for the 2026 ADA thresholds.
  • Fryar CD, et al. Anthropometric Reference Data for Children and Adults: United States, August 2021–August 2023 (NCHS, Vital and Health Statistics Series 3, No. 50, 2025): the published averages our decade figures match.
  • CDC NHANES 2011–2018 whole-body DXA scans: our calculation of body fat at each BMI for women aged 20 to 59 (the body fat percentage chart has the method).
  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the cut-offs and the “regardless of age, sex, or race” rule.
  • CDC. Calculating BMI: the formula in US units.
  • CDC. Child and Teen BMI Categories: percentiles under 20.
  • NHLBI. Clinical Guidelines on the Identification, Evaluation, and Treatment of Overweight and Obesity in Adults (NIH, 1998): the waist cut-offs for women and men.
  • NICE. Overweight and obesity management, guideline NG246 (2025): waist-to-height ratio classes and the lower BMI thresholds by family background.
  • American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee for Obesity. Screening, Diagnosis, Evaluation, and Staging of Obesity in Adults: Standards of Care in Overweight and Obesity—2026: obesity thresholds with waist size (recommendations 3.3a and 3.3b) and the note on older adults.
  • CDC. National Diabetes Prevention Program eligibility: BMI of 23 or higher for Asian American adults.
  • WHO Expert Consultation. Appropriate body-mass index for Asian populations (Lancet, 2004).
  • Winter JE, et al. BMI and all-cause mortality in older adults: a meta-analysis (Am J Clin Nutr, 2014).
  • CDC. Weight Gain During Pregnancy: recommended gain by BMI before pregnancy (Institute of Medicine).

Method. Women aged 20 and over with a measured height and weight in NHANES August 2021–August 2023, pregnant women excluded, weighted with CDC’s exam weights. Percentiles come from the weighted distribution of the measured BMIs (one weight per distinct BMI, straight lines in between), the method that reproduces NCHS’s published medians; the rank reads your BMI off those percentiles. Shares carry 95% confidence intervals from Taylor linearization over the survey’s 15 strata, and the page states a difference between ages only when its interval excludes zero. Every figure is rounded once, from the unrounded estimate, with one exception: the rank reads percentile curves stored to two decimals, the homepage’s, which moves a few results by one point. The waist check rounds your waist to 0.1 inch or 0.1 cm and cuts the ratio to two decimals instead of rounding it (0.4953 shows as 0.49), so 0.50 always means a waist of at least half the height; it compares what it prints and uses your BMI as the calculator shows it. US guidelines give their waist line as 35 inches and as 88 cm; the check uses the one in your unit.
