Body Fat Calculator

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Estimate your body fat percentage four ways and see how close each method comes to a DXA scan.

Accuracy: our check on CDC’s scans of 10,864 US adults (2011–2018) and published studies. Method and sources

Key takeaways

A body fat calculator estimates your body fat from a few measurements, and the estimate can miss. On CDC’s DXA scans of US adults, the waist-and-height formula came within 5 points of the scan for 86% of men and 74% of women, and the height-and-weight formula for 66% and 62% (our calculation).

  • The methods disagree for the same person: the woman in the worked example below gets 33.0% by tape measure, 33.5% by height and weight and 38.4% by waist and height.
  • The waist-and-height formula reads high on average, by 2.3 points for women and 0.7 for men. The height-and-weight formula reads low at a healthy BMI and high with obesity.
  • CDC’s survey measured neither the neck nor skinfolds. In published studies, the tape method read 6.0 points below DXA on average in Army recruits, and seven-site skinfolds 1.4 points below in college students.
  • Healthy body fat ranges are provisional: 21–32.9% for women and 8–19.9% for men aged 20 to 39 (Gallagher et al., 2000).

Medical disclaimer: This calculator gives estimates for information only. A body fat estimate is not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

How to read your result

Every number here uses the calculator’s starting values: a woman of 35, 5′4″ (162.6 cm) and 150 lb (68.0 kg), with a neck of 13, a waist of 31 and hips of 40 inches for the tape measure. For waist and height, whose field starts empty, the example adds a 34-inch waist at the hip bones.

Body fat: 33.0%, by tape measure
163.205 × log10(31 + 40 − 13) − 97.684 × log10(64) − 78.387 = 33.0, the women’s tape equation, in inches.
High on the provisional range
For women aged 20 to 39, the provisional ranges call 21–32.9% healthy and 33–38.9% high (Gallagher et al., 2000), so 33.0% is high: the high range starts at 33%. The result card is tinted to match.
How close: checked in Army recruits, not on CDC’s scans
CDC’s survey didn’t measure the neck. In 1,407 Army recruits, the tape method read 6.0 points below DXA on average (Foulis et al., 2023).
Where 33.0% falls: 79% of US women aged 30 to 39 measured more
On CDC’s scans, 79% of US women aged 30 to 39 had more body fat than 33.0%; their median was 38.9%.
Fat and lean mass: 49.5 lb and 100.5 lb
150 lb × 33.0% = 49.5 lb of fat (22.5 kg); the other 100.5 lb (45.6 kg) is lean mass.
Other estimates: 33.5% from height and weight
Her BMI is 25.7 (150 × 703 ÷ 64²), and 1.20 × 25.7 + 0.23 × 35 − 5.4 = 33.5. Among US women whose height-and-weight estimate was within a point of 34%, CDC’s scans read 34.0% to 39.5% for the middle half, median 36.8% (446 women).
Waist and height: 38.4% with a 34-inch waist
Measured at the top of the hip bones, a waist of 34 inches gives 76 − 20 × 64 ÷ 34 = 38.4. Among US women whose estimate was within a point of 38%, the scans read 33.7% to 38.5%, median 36.1% (543 women). The two formulas disagree by 4.9 points, yet women with either estimate had scans with a median near 36% to 37%.

How accurate is each method?

Each method predicts body fat from something easier to measure, and the prediction can miss. To see by how much, we applied two of the formulas to CDC’s DXA scans of US adults aged 20 to 59, taken from 2011 to 2018 in its National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). CDC’s documentation calls DXA “the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition”. The survey measured height, weight and waist, but not the neck or skinfolds, so the tape and caliper methods rest on published studies.

  • Tape measure (neck, waist, hips for women, height): in 1,407 US Army recruits, 6.0 points below DXA on average.
  • Waist and height: on CDC’s scans of 10,751 US adults, within 5 points for 74% of women and 86% of men.
  • Height and weight: on CDC’s scans of 10,829 US adults, within 5 points for 62% of women and 66% of men.
  • Calipers (3 or 7 skinfolds): in 437 college students, seven sites read 1.4 points below DXA on average; the average miss was 11.7% of the scan’s reading.

The waist-and-height formula came closer. In the same people, it came within 5 points of the scan for 12 more women in every 100 than the height-and-weight formula, and for 20 more men. Its typical miss was 3.5 points for women and 2.7 for men, against 4.7 and 4.2 (our calculation; all four differences are outside their margins of error).

Our calculation from CDC NHANES 2011–2018: 5,388 women and 5,363 men aged 20 to 59 with a measured waist, 5,446 and 5,383 with a BMI. Typical miss: the average distance from the scan, either way. Average error: formula minus scan. BMI groups follow CDC’s adult categories, from 18.5 up. In the group rows, every average error is outside its margin of error except waist and height for men with a healthy BMI (+0.1); overall, the height-and-weight formula’s average errors (−0.2 and −0.1) are within theirs.

The waist-and-height formula reads high. Its median error, the measure its authors report, was +2.3 points for women and +0.8 for men. On the 2005–2006 scans they tested it on (adults aged 20 to 69), the median errors were 0.9 and 0.5 points (Woolcott and Bergman, 2018). For women it read clearly higher on the newer scans (the two 95% confidence intervals don’t overlap); for men the two don’t clearly differ. The samples also differ in scanner model (Hologic Discovery A, against the QDR 4500A) and age range, so the reason isn’t known.

The height-and-weight formula misses by group. Averaged over everyone it comes out about right (an average error of −0.2 points for women and −0.1 for men, within its margin of error), but only because its misses cancel out. At a healthy BMI it read 3.1 points low for women and 2.9 for men; with obesity, 4.1 and 2.9 points high. It also read about 2 points low under age 40 and about 2 points high from 40 to 59.

The tape measure and calipers. In 1,407 US Army recruits, the tape method read 6.0 points below DXA on average, in women and in men, at the start of basic training (Foulis et al., 2023). In 437 college students, seven-site skinfolds read 1.4 points below DXA on average, and their average miss, 11.7% of the scan’s reading, was the lowest of the seven methods tested (Burns et al., 2019). Both studies were of young adults; neither covered older ages.

Tape measure (the Navy method)

Hodgdon and Beckett, at the US Naval Health Research Center, worked out these equations in 1984 from the neck, the waist and the height, plus the hips for women. The same equations appear in the US Army’s sample body fat calculations (Army Table B-5). They take inches; the calculator converts centimeters first.

  • Men: 86.010 × log10(waist − neck) − 70.041 × log10(height) + 36.76
  • Women: 163.205 × log10(waist + hips − neck) − 97.684 × log10(height) − 78.387

Where to measure, as on the Army’s 2013 worksheets (DA Form 5500 and 5501):

  • Neck: just below the Adam’s apple.
  • Waist, men: at the navel.
  • Waist, women: at the narrowest point.
  • Hips, women: where the buttocks stick out most.

The worksheets take each measurement three times and average them.

The Army’s worked examples: a woman with a 15-inch neck, 42-inch waist, 44-inch hips and a height of 64 inches, and a man with a 16-inch neck, 49-inch waist and a height of 69 inches. Worked by hand with rounded logarithms, the document gets 46.73% and 38.62% and reports 47% and 39%. Computed exactly, they are 47.3% and 38.6%, which round to the same.

Against DXA, the tape method read 6.0 points low on average in Army recruits, with a standard deviation of 4.4 points for women and 3.5 for men (Foulis et al., 2023). In June 2023 the Army moved to a one-site tape test (Army Directive 2023-11). For a military body composition check, follow your service’s own procedure; this calculator is for information.

Waist and height (relative fat mass)

Woolcott and Bergman built relative fat mass (RFM) on CDC’s DXA scans of 12,581 adults from 1999–2004 and tested it on 3,456 from 2005–2006 (Sci Rep, 2018). It needs one tape measurement and your height, in the same unit:

  • Men: 64 − 20 × height ÷ waist
  • Women: 76 − 20 × height ÷ waist

Measure the waist at the top of your hip bones (Cedars-Sinai). CDC’s survey measures it there, at the top of the right hip bone (Woolcott and Bergman, 2018). The tape method uses another spot (the navel for men, the narrowest point for women), so the two waists can differ.

On CDC’s newer scans, from 2011–2018, it came within 5 points for 74% of women and 86% of men, with a typical miss of 3.5 and 2.7 points, and read 2.3 and 0.7 points high on average (our calculation).

Height and weight (the Deurenberg formula)

Deurenberg and colleagues measured body fat from body density in 1,229 people aged 7 to 83 and fitted a formula for adults from BMI, age and sex. Its standard error of estimate is 4.1 points, and its authors found it slightly overestimates body fat in people with obesity (Br J Nutr, 1991):

Body fat % = 1.20 × BMI + 0.23 × age − 10.8 × sex − 5.4, with sex 1 for men and 0 for women.

It needs no tape, but it starts from BMI, so it can’t tell muscle from fat. Children 15 and under have a formula of their own in the same paper.

Checked on CDC’s 2011–2018 scans, it came within 5 points for 62% of women and 66% of men, with a typical miss of 4.7 and 4.2 points. It read 3.1 points low for women with a healthy BMI and 4.1 high for women with obesity (our calculation; all groups).

Skinfold calipers (Jackson–Pollock)

Calipers measure a pinch of skin and the fat under it. Jackson and Pollock’s equations turn the sum of the pinches, in millimeters, and your age into body density, and Siri’s equation turns density into body fat.

  • 3 sites, men: chest, abdomen and thigh.
  • 3 sites, women: triceps, suprailiac (just above the hip bone) and thigh.
  • 7 sites, both: chest, midaxillary (side of the chest), triceps, subscapular (below the shoulder blade), abdomen, suprailiac and thigh.
  • 3 sites, men: density = 1.10938 − 0.0008267 × S + 0.0000016 × S² − 0.0002574 × age
  • 3 sites, women: density = 1.0994921 − 0.0009929 × S + 0.0000023 × S² − 0.0001392 × age
  • 7 sites, men: density = 1.112 − 0.00043499 × S + 0.00000055 × S² − 0.00028826 × age
  • 7 sites, women: density = 1.097 − 0.00046971 × S + 0.00000056 × S² − 0.00012828 × age
  • Body fat (Siri): 495 ÷ density − 450

S is the sum of the skinfolds in millimeters. Coefficients as printed by Topend Sports, citing Jackson and Pollock (1978) and Jackson, Pollock and Ward (1980). Serra et al., ConScientiae Saúde 2009 print the same equations, but the women’s 3-site age term as 0.0001395 instead of 0.0001392, which changes the result by less than 0.02 points at any age from 18 to 79.

In 437 college students, seven-site skinfolds read 1.4 points below DXA on average; the average miss was 11.7% of the scan’s reading (Burns et al., 2019).

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC NHANES 2011–2018 whole-body DXA scans with the demographics and body measures files (DXX, DEMO, BMX, cycles G to J): our calculation for 10,864 adults aged 20 to 59, 10,751 with a measured waist and 10,829 with a BMI. The documentation calls DXA “the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition”.

  • Cedars-Sinai. Building a Better Body Measurement: Relative Fat Mass (where to measure the waist).
  • Hodgdon JA, Beckett MB. Prediction of percent body fat for U.S. Navy men and women from body circumferences and height. Naval Health Research Center reports 84-11 and 84-29, 1984, as reproduced in the US Army’s sample calculations (Table B-5) in study protocol NCT02734238.
  • US Army. Body fat content worksheets DA Form 5500 (male) and DA Form 5501 (female), May 2013: where to measure.

  • Deurenberg P, Weststrate JA, Seidell JC. Body mass index as a measure of body fatness: age- and sex-specific prediction formulas. Br J Nutr 1991;65:105–14.
  • Jackson AS, Pollock ML. Br J Nutr 1978;40:497–504; Jackson AS, Pollock ML, Ward A. Med Sci Sports Exerc 1980;12:175–81; Siri WE, 1956; as printed by Topend Sports (Jackson-Pollock body density) and in Serra AJ, et al. ConScientiae Saúde 2009;8(1):19–24.
  • US Army. Army Directive 2023-11, June 2023: the one-site tape test.
  • Burns RD, Fu Y, Constantino N. Measurement agreement in percent body fat estimates among laboratory and field assessments in college students: Use of equivalence testing. PLOS ONE 2019;14(3):e0214029.
  • Gallagher D, et al. Healthy percentage body fat ranges. Am J Clin Nutr 2000;72:694–701, as tabulated in J Public Health Afr 2016, Table 1.

Method. Adults aged 20 to 59 with a valid whole-body DXA scan in NHANES 2011–2012 to 2017–2018, pregnant women excluded, weighted with CDC’s exam weights divided by four. For each person we worked out the height-and-weight formula from their measured BMI and age, and relative fat mass from their measured height and waist, then subtracted their scan. Shares, typical misses and average errors are survey-weighted, with 95% confidence intervals from Taylor linearization over the survey’s 59 strata and 121 primary sampling units; the page states a difference only when its interval excludes zero. The two formulas are compared in the same people. For the estimate checks, we took the people whose formula gave an estimate within a point of each whole percent (at least 30 of them) and read the middle half and median of their scans (weighted percentiles by the midpoint rule, as on the body fat percentage chart). Every figure is rounded once, from the unrounded estimate. The US comparison and the provisional ranges are the body fat percentage chart’s.
