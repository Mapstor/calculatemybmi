Childhood Obesity in America

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Data: CDC National Center for Health Statistics, published February 2026 · Reviewed October 2, 2026 · Not medical advice

Every measured CDC number on children’s weight in one place: how many kids have obesity, how that changed since the 1960s, and how it differs by age, sex and race. All of it comes from kids who were actually weighed and measured, not from parents’ estimates.

21.1% of US children and teens ages 2–19 had obesity in August 2021–August 2023, the latest measured CDC data — about 1 in 5 kids, or roughly 15.8 million. That includes 7.0% with severe obesity, and another 15.1% were overweight. Teens 12–19 (22.9%) and children 6–11 (22.1%) had higher rates than ages 2–5 (14.9%).

Source: CDC National Center for Health Statistics, Health E-Stat 112 (February 2026). Millions: our calculation using the NHANES population total for ages 2–19.

Childhood obesity rate by year, 1963–2023

Obesity among US children and teens has roughly quadrupled since the early 1970s, from 5.2% in 1971–1974 to 21.1% in 2021–2023. Severe obesity rose even faster, from 1.0% to 7.0%. Tap the buttons to add or remove groups; tap a point for its value and margin of error.

Childhood obesity by age: preschoolers, kids and teens

Obesity climbs once children reach school age. In 2021–2023 it was 14.9% among ages 2–5, 22.1% among ages 6–11 and 22.9% among teens 12–19. The teen figure is the closest thing to a “teenage obesity rate”: roughly 2 in 9 teens. Teen boys stood out at 26.0%, against 19.6% for teen girls.

Boys vs girls

Boys are more likely to have obesity; girls are more likely to be overweight. In 2021–2023, 23.0% of boys and 19.1% of girls ages 2–19 had obesity, while 17.5% of girls and 13.0% of boys were overweight. Severe obesity: 7.8% of boys, 6.3% of girls. Those are survey estimates with margins of error of a few points, so treat small gaps with care.

Childhood obesity by race and Hispanic origin

Rates differ widely by race and Hispanic origin, but the newest survey can’t pin every group down. CDC’s 2021–2023 sample no longer oversampled Hispanic and Asian children, so several estimates miss its reliability standard. Among the reliable 2021–2023 figures, Hispanic boys were at 29.8% and white boys at 18.7%; Black girls were at 29.9%, Hispanic girls 23.1% and white girls 15.7%. The 2017–2018 column, from a survey with full oversampling, shows the same broad pattern.

Is childhood obesity increasing?

Over decades, clearly yes; over the last few years, the survey can’t say for sure. The rate rose from 5.2% in 1971–1974 to 13.9% in 1999–2000 and 19.3% in 2017–2018, then 21.1% in 2021–2023. The last step is within the margins of error: our approximate 95% ranges are 17.3–21.3% for 2017–2018 and 18.9–23.3% for 2021–2023, and they overlap. The survey also changed design after its pandemic pause, which makes close comparisons harder.

For adults, the same pattern shows up in our US obesity statistics: a long climb, and recent figures that are high but not clearly still rising.

How obesity is defined for children

Children aren’t judged by the adult BMI cutoffs. Their BMI is compared with other children of the same age and sex using CDC growth charts: obesity is a BMI at or above the 95th percentile, severe obesity is at or above 120% of the 95th percentile, and overweight is the 85th to just under the 95th percentile (CDC child and teen BMI categories). CDC also cites research suggesting that, at a given BMI, BMI overstates body fat among Black children and teens.

FAQ

What percentage of children in America have obesity?

21.1% of children and teens ages 2–19 had obesity in August 2021–August 2023, including 7.0% with severe obesity (CDC, measured).

How many kids in the US have obesity?

About 15.8 million children and teens ages 2–19, our calculation from CDC’s 21.1% and the survey’s population total of about 74.9 million kids.

What is the teenage obesity rate?

22.9% of teens ages 12–19 had obesity in 2021–2023: 26.0% of boys and 19.6% of girls.

Is childhood obesity going up?

It has risen about 4.1-fold since 1971–1974. The rise from 19.3% (2017–2018) to 21.1% (2021–2023) is within the survey’s margins of error.

Is there childhood obesity data by state?

Not on this page yet: state child data come from a different, parent-reported survey that we haven’t verified. Adult state rates are on our obesity rate by state page.

Methodology and sources

Every percentage on this page is transcribed from NCHS Health E-Stat 112 (February 2026), which reports measured heights and weights from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and its predecessors since 1963–1965, and was checked against the published tables. Pregnant girls are excluded by CDC. Underweight (4.4%) comes from Health E-Stat 119. Values labelled “calculated” are ours: counts of children (rate × the NHANES population total for ages 2–19, 74,908,988), the healthy-weight share in the figure, combined overweight-or-obesity, and approximate 95% ranges (estimate ± 1.96 standard errors).
