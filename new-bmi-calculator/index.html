Key takeaways

New BMI is Nick Trefethen’s 2013 alternative to the traditional formula, proposed while he was a professor at the University of Oxford: 1.3 × kg ÷ m2.5, or 5734 × lb ÷ in2.5. It matches traditional BMI at about 5′6.5″ (1.69 m), reads higher if you are shorter and lower if you are taller.

  • The gap depends on height alone: New BMI is 5.3% higher than traditional BMI at 5′0″ and 3.9% lower at 6′0″, whatever you weigh.
  • A traditional BMI of 25.0 becomes 26.3 at 5′0″ and 24.0 at 6′0″.
  • Trefethen says his formula is “not based on epidemiological studies” and may not be an improvement.
  • CDC’s formula uses height squared and its cut-offs are set on that number, so use traditional BMI for anything medical.

Medical disclaimer: This calculator gives estimates for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

The two formulas

Traditional BMI and New BMI
FormulaUS unitsMetric
Traditional BMIlb ÷ in² × 703kg ÷ m²
New BMI5734 × lb ÷ in2.51.3 × kg ÷ m2.5

The constants keep the reading unchanged at what Trefethen calls “average height, which I take to be about 66.5 inches, i.e., 1.69 meters”. The square root of 1.69 is 1.3, so at that height New BMI and traditional BMI are the same number.

Divide one formula by the other and weight cancels: New BMI = traditional BMI × 1.3 ÷ √height (m). Below 1.69 m that factor is above 1, so New BMI comes out higher; above 1.69 m it is below 1, so it comes out lower.

Two worked examples

5′2″ and 150 lb (157.5 cm, 68.0 kg)

Traditional BMI: 27.4, Overweight
150 × 703 ÷ 62² = 27.4. In metric, 68.04 ÷ 1.5748² gives the same 27.4.
New BMI: 28.4, Overweight
5734 × 150 ÷ 622.5 = 28.4. In metric, 1.3 × 68.04 ÷ 1.57482.5 = 28.4.
Difference: +1.0
28.4 − 27.4. Both numbers are overweight, so the category stays the same.

6′2″ and 150 lb (188.0 cm, 68.0 kg)

Traditional BMI: 19.3, Healthy weight
150 × 703 ÷ 74² = 19.3. In metric, 68.04 ÷ 1.8796² gives the same 19.3.
New BMI: 18.3, Underweight
5734 × 150 ÷ 742.5 = 18.3. In metric, 1.3 × 68.04 ÷ 1.87962.5 = 18.3.
Difference: −1.0
18.3 − 19.3. The category changes from Healthy weight to Underweight: New BMI drops below CDC’s 18.5 cut-off. CDC set that cut-off on traditional BMI, and on CDC’s scale this person, at 19.3, is a healthy weight.

Why Trefethen proposed it

In a letter to The Economist published on 5 January 2013, signed as professor of numerical analysis at the University of Oxford, Nick Trefethen called BMI “a bizarre measure”. He wrote: “We live in a three-dimensional world, yet the BMI is defined as weight divided by height squared.” His conclusion was that “millions of short people think they are thinner than they are, and millions of tall people think they are fatter.”

The formula is on his Oxford page, where he explains the exponent: “I propose that a better approximation to the actual sizes and shapes of healthy bodies might be given by an exponent of 2.5.”

On the same page he sets out the limits of his proposal. “I am an applied mathematician, not a doctor or an epidemiologist,” he writes, and the new formulas are “not based on epidemiological studies” and may not be an improvement. He points to a 2007 paper by Heymsfield and colleagues in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition that supports the exponent 2.

Which number to use

For anything medical, use traditional BMI. CDC calculates BMI as weight (kg) ÷ height (m)², or weight (lb) ÷ height (in)² × 703, and sets its adult categories on that number: under 18.5 underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 healthy weight, 25 to 29.9 overweight and 30 or more obesity. WHO uses the same two lines for adults: overweight at a BMI of 25 or more, obesity at 30 or more.

New BMI is worth a second look if you are very short or very tall, where the formulas part most: 5.3% higher at 5′0″, 6.4% lower at 6′4″. The calculator applies CDC’s cut-offs to both numbers so you can compare them.

Neither formula can separate muscle from fat. CDC notes that “BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass”, and New BMI works from the same height and weight. The body fat calculator estimates body fat with a tape measure or calipers, and body fat vs BMI covers how the two measures differ.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we worked the figure out from the two formulas with the method below.

  • Trefethen N. New BMI (New Body Mass Index), University of Oxford: the formula in metric and US units, the constants, the 2.5 exponent and his caveats.
  • Trefethen N. Letter, The Economist, 5 January 2013.
  • CDC. Calculating BMI: the formula in metric and US units.
  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the cut-offs.
  • CDC. About Body Mass Index (BMI): what BMI does not measure.
  • WHO. Obesity and overweight, fact sheet: the adult definitions of overweight and obesity.

Method. Every number in the tables, the figure and the examples is our calculation with the calculator’s own arithmetic: traditional BMI = lb ÷ in² × 703 (kg ÷ m² for metric entries) and New BMI = 1.3 × kg ÷ m2.5, with kg = lb ÷ 2.20462 and m = in × 0.0254. Each BMI is rounded once to one decimal, halves up, and its category comes from that rounded value. The difference subtracts the two rounded values, so it matches what the calculator prints. The percentages are 1.3 ÷ √height (m) − 1, rounded to 0.1%. The figure plots the unrounded difference at 70 kg every 0.01 m from 1.40 to 2.00 m. Trefethen’s 5734 rounds the US-unit constant, so working in pounds and inches with it can move the last decimal near a rounding edge; both worked examples give the same result either way.
