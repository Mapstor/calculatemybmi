Body Fat vs BMI: The Complete Guide to Understanding Both Metrics

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

BMI = Weight (kg) / Height (m)2 World Health Organization BMI Classification

For example, a person weighing 70 kg who is 1.75 m tall would have a BMI of 22.9 (70 / 1.752). This falls within the healthy weight category.

CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure”, but it does not directly measure body fat (CDC). Body fat percentage does, and the two can disagree; our BMI limitations guide covers the rest.

What Is Body Fat Percentage?

Body fat percentage represents the proportion of your total body weight that consists of fat tissue. Unlike BMI, which does not directly measure body fat (CDC), it is a measure of fat itself. To see typical values by age and sex, see our body fat percentage chart, built from CDC body scans. To estimate yours, use our body fat calculator.

Where fat sits matters too. NIH calls excess abdominal fat an important, independent risk factor for disease (NHLBI, 1998), and NCI says visceral fat, around the organs, seems to carry more disease risk than overall or subcutaneous fat (NCI).

Healthy Ranges: BMI vs Body Fat Percentage

BMI uses one set of categories for all adults (CDC). Body fat had no accepted published ranges when, in 2000, Gallagher and colleagues proposed provisional ones by translating the healthy BMI range into predicted body fat by age, sex and ethnic group (Gallagher et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2000). For African American and white adults aged 20 to 39, the healthy range they proposed is 21 to 32.9% for women and 8 to 19.9% for men (Table 4); our body fat percentage chart has every age.

For more detailed information about healthy BMI ranges, see our guides on BMI for women and BMI for men.

How Each Metric Is Measured

Measuring BMI

BMI measurement is straightforward and requires only two data points:

  1. Weight: Measured on a scale, in light clothing
  2. Height: Measured standing straight against a wall or using a stadiometer

Our BMI calculator works it out and gives your CDC category and the healthy weight range for your height.

Measuring Body Fat

CDC calls DXA “the most widely accepted method of measuring body composition” (CDC); tape measures, calipers and body fat scales estimate body fat from other measurements. Our body fat calculator works from a tape measure, calipers, or height and weight.

When BMI and Body Fat Tell Different Stories

BMI and body fat can disagree in three ways.

Scenario 1: Normal BMI, High Body Fat ("Skinny Fat")

Also called “normal weight obesity”: a BMI in the healthy range with a high body fat percentage. BMI does not directly measure body fat (CDC), so it can’t show this. A body fat estimate can, and a waist-to-height ratio shows the fat around the waist.

Scenario 2: High BMI, Low Body Fat (Muscular Individuals)

The opposite: BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle and bone mass (CDC), so very muscular people can reach an overweight or obese BMI with little body fat. At the NFL Scouting Combine, BMI classed 53.4% of prospective players as obese, against 8.9% by measured body fat (Provencher et al., 2018).

Our BMI for athletes guide covers the research.

Scenario 3: Similar BMI, Very Different Health

Two men aged 20 to 39 with the same BMI can sit in different body fat ranges:

15% Body Fat
Person A (BMI 26)
30% Body Fat
Person B (BMI 26)

Both have a BMI of 26, overweight. On the provisional ranges for men aged 20 to 39, 15% body fat is healthy (8 to 19.9%) and 30% is very high (25% or more) (Gallagher et al., 2000).

Why Athletes Often Have High BMI But Low Body Fat

Muscle adds weight, and BMI does not distinguish it from fat (CDC), so a muscular athlete can have a high BMI and a low body fat percentage. Body fat percentage and lean body mass add what BMI leaves out; BMI for athletes covers the research.

Accuracy Comparison: BMI vs Body Fat Percentage

BMI Accuracy Issues

BMI has several accuracy problems:

BMI Limitations

  • Cannot distinguish between muscle and fat mass
  • Doesn't account for fat distribution (visceral vs subcutaneous)
  • Same scale for men and women despite different body compositions
  • Body fat at the same BMI rises with age
  • May overestimate obesity in muscular individuals
  • Doesn't show blood pressure or cholesterol

BMI Strengths

  • Quick and low-cost
  • A reliable screening measure
  • The same categories for every adult
  • Death rates rise at both ends of the scale in pooled data

Sources: CDC, About BMI; CDC, adult categories; NIH reads the waist alongside BMI (NHLBI, 1998); Gallagher et al., 2000; Provencher et al., 2018; Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016.

Our BMI limitations guide goes through each one.

Body Fat Percentage Accuracy

In a study of 437 college students that tested seven methods against DXA, seven-site skinfolds read 1.4 points below DXA on average and a hand-held impedance device was 4.9 points off (Burns et al., 2019); our guide to how to measure body fat has the rest.

Which Metric Is Better for Health Assessment?

Each answers a different question. CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure” and says that, for individuals, it should be considered with other factors, such as blood pressure and cholesterol levels (CDC). When to use which:

BMI

Use BMI When:

You need a quick screen, have no way to measure body fat, or are comparing with US or WHO figures, which use BMI.

%

Use Body Fat % When:

You’re very muscular, tracking body composition, or 65 or older, an age at which NICE advises caution in reading BMI (NICE NG246).

Both

Use Both When:

You want both answers. NIH also reads the waist alongside BMI from 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI, 1998).

Additional metrics to consider include waist-to-height ratio, which NICE uses to assess central adiposity, and lean body mass, which tracks your muscle tissue specifically.

Using Both Metrics Together

When the two disagree, the scenarios above show why. Beside them, also check:

  • Waist circumference: NIH marks higher risk above 40 in (102 cm) for men and 35 in (88 cm) for women, at a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI, 1998)
  • Waist-to-height ratio: 0.5 or more means increased central adiposity (NICE)
  • Blood pressure and cholesterol: CDC lists them among the factors to consider with BMI (CDC)
