How to Lower BMI: CDC’s Steps for Healthy Weight Loss

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

BMI is weight divided by height squared, so for an adult it falls only when weight does. CDC describes healthy weight loss as a lifestyle with healthy eating patterns, regular physical activity, enough sleep and stress management (CDC). Below: how much weight one BMI point is at your height, what the official guidance says on each of those four, and CDC’s five steps for getting started.

Key Takeaways
  • Lose weight gradually: CDC notes that people who lose about 1 to 2 pounds a week are more likely to keep the weight off (CDC)
  • The 3,500 kcal rule overstates long-term loss: Hall and colleagues’ model gives about 1 pound of eventual weight change for every 10 kcal a day of change in intake, half of it in about a year (Hall 2011)
  • Eating and activity: most weight loss comes from eating fewer calories, and regular physical activity is the only way to keep it off, CDC says (CDC)
  • Even modest loss helps: if you are not at a healthy weight, CDC says even modest weight loss can help improve blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar levels; 5% of 200 pounds is 10 pounds (CDC)
  • Healthy weight: a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 for adults (CDC; see our healthy BMI guide)

Understanding BMI and Weight Loss

CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure” for underweight, overweight and obesity, though it does not directly measure body fat (CDC).

The BMI formula is straightforward: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. For those using imperial measurements, the formula is (weight in pounds / height in inches squared) x 703. You can quickly check your current BMI using our BMI calculator.

Creating a Calorie Deficit: The Foundation of Weight Loss

More physical activity raises the number of calories your body uses, and using calories through activity while eating fewer creates a calorie deficit that results in weight loss, CDC says. Most of the loss, it adds, comes from eating fewer calories (CDC).

The 3,500 kcal rule — and why it overstates long-term loss

The traditional teaching is that about 3,500 kcal equals a pound of body fat, so cutting 500 kcal a day takes off about a pound a week. Hall and colleagues showed that this rule ignores how the body adapts: as weight falls, so does the energy it burns, and the response to a change in intake is slow, with half times of about a year (Hall et al., Lancet 2011). Their rule of thumb for an average overweight adult is about 1 pound of eventual weight change for every 10 kcal a day of change in intake, with half of it reached in about a year and 95% in about three years. NIDDK’s Body Weight Planner is built on the same model.

Eating for a Healthy Weight

CDC says an eating plan that helps promote health and manage weight includes a variety of nutrient-dense foods. Citing the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, it describes a healthy eating plan as one that (CDC):

  • Emphasizes vegetables, fruits, protein, dairy without added sugars, healthy fats and whole grains
  • Includes protein foods such as seafood, meats, poultry, eggs, legumes (beans and peas), soy products, nuts and seeds
  • Is low in added sugars, sodium (salt), saturated fats, trans fats and cholesterol
  • Stays within your daily calorie needs

Swaps CDC Suggests

  • Drinks: water instead of sugary drinks, the leading source of added sugars in the American diet; a 12-ounce regular soda has about 155 calories and 10 teaspoons of sugar (CDC)
  • Cooking: baking or grilling instead of frying fish or breading chicken, and beans in place of meat (CDC)
  • Fruit: canned fruit packed in water or its own juice, since dried and canned fruit may contain added sugars or syrups (CDC)
  • Vegetables: frozen and canned vegetables without added salt (CDC)
  • Alcohol: it counts too, at about 7 calories per gram (MedlinePlus)

Exercise Recommendations for Lowering BMI

How Much Activity the Guidelines Call For

The federal Physical Activity Guidelines say adults need at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, and muscle-strengthening activity, like lifting weights or doing push-ups, at least 2 days each week (Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans).

For weight, CDC adds that you may need more than that to maintain your weight, and that the amount varies greatly from person to person. To lose weight and keep it off, you will need a high amount of activity unless you also cut the calories you eat and drink (CDC).

What Regular Activity Does

CDC lists stronger bones and muscles, better brain health and a better ability to do everyday activities among the benefits. Physical activity also helps improve sleep quality, it says, and reduce high blood pressure, the risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, several forms of cancer, osteoporosis and falls, arthritis pain, and symptoms of depression and anxiety (CDC).

CDC’s Five Steps for Losing Weight

If you want to lose weight, CDC says making a specific plan can help, and it sets out five steps for getting started (CDC):

Understand Your “Why”

Write down why you want to lose weight, whether heart disease runs in your family or you want to be more active, and keep the list where you’ll see it every day.

Track Where You Are

For a few days, write down or log in an app everything you eat and drink, your physical activity (time of day, activity, duration), your hours of sleep and the healthy ways you’re reducing stress. In a 20-week group program for 1,685 adults, who lost 5.8 kg on average, the food records people kept and their physical activity accounted for most of the differences in how much they lost (Hollis et al., 2008).

Set Specific Goals

Pick two or three short-term goals at a time, such as water instead of sugary drinks, a 15-minute evening walk or a vegetable with supper. Setting Realistic Goals has CDC’s test for a specific goal.

Find Support

Family and friends who back your efforts, or coworkers and neighbors with similar goals who might share recipes and plan group activities. CDC says it may help to join a weight-loss program or visit a health care professional, such as a nutrition or weight-loss specialist (CDC).

Monitor Your Progress

Revisit your goals regularly: keep the parts of your plan that work, change the rest, add a new goal once you’re meeting one consistently, and reward yourself with something other than food (CDC).

Everyday Obstacles

CDC’s examples of lifestyle challenges, with ways around them (CDC):

  • Work makes it hard to be active: walk to work or take public transportation, take walk breaks, or ask a coworker to walk with you at lunch or after work
  • Sugary foods bought for the kids: keep fruits and vegetables on hand to grab, make healthier changes as a family, and plan meals ahead
  • Coworkers bring in treats: bring healthier snacks to share

Sleep and Stress Management

CDC counts enough sleep and stress management as part of healthy weight loss, and names stress among the factors that can affect weight management, alongside medicines, medical conditions, genes, hormones, environment and age (CDC).

The Sleep-Weight Connection

Sleep helps keep the hunger hormone ghrelin and the fullness hormone leptin in balance. Without enough sleep, ghrelin goes up and leptin goes down, and you feel hungrier than when you’re well rested. Sleep deficiency also leaves blood sugar higher than normal, which may raise the risk of diabetes (NHLBI).

If you’re tired because you’re not getting enough sleep, CDC suggests regular bed and wake times, no caffeine in the afternoon and evening, and turning off electronic devices at least 30 minutes before bedtime (CDC).

Setting Realistic Goals

CDC warns that unrealistic goals, such as losing 20 pounds in 2 weeks, can leave you feeling defeated and frustrated (CDC).

What Makes a Goal Specific

CDC’s advice is two or three short-term goals at a time, each specific and realistic, with a reward along the way (CDC):

Not specific: “Exercise more.”
Specific, and potentially more realistic: “I will walk 15 minutes, 3 days a week for the first week.”

Occasional setbacks are expected, CDC says: get back on track as quickly as you can, then think about how to prevent the same setback next time. To read your targets, see our BMI categories guide.

Tracking Progress Beyond the Scale

Alongside weight and BMI, NIH and CDC give three more things to watch:

1

Waist Size

NIH’s 1998 guidelines mark higher risk above 40 in (102 cm) for men and 35 in (88 cm) for women, used with BMI (NHLBI, 1998). Our tracking guide shows how to measure it.

2

Health Markers

If you are not at a healthy weight, even modest weight loss can help improve blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar levels, CDC says (CDC).

3

Sleep and Activity

CDC’s tracking step logs your hours of sleep and each activity’s time of day and duration (CDC), and better sleep quality is among the effects of regular activity CDC lists (CDC).

When Weight Loss Plateaus

As weight falls, so does the energy the body burns, so the same intake brings less loss over time. In Hall and colleagues’ model, half of the eventual change from a new intake comes in about a year and 95% in about three years (Hall 2011).

Why Plateaus Happen

  • Lower energy needs: a smaller body burns less, so the deficit you started with shrinks (Hall 2011)
  • Weekly swings: in 80 adults weighed daily, weight ran highest on Sunday and Monday and fell through the week; the researchers advise reading weekend-to-weekday swings as normal, not as weight gain (Orsama et al., 2014)

What to Do About a Plateau

Recalculate From Your Current Weight

NIDDK’s Body Weight Planner works out a new calorie and activity plan from where you are now.

Track Everything for a Few Days

A food and beverage diary shows what you eat and drink and where small changes could go, CDC says (CDC).

Revisit Your Goals

Decide which parts of your plan work and which need changing, then revise your goals to match (CDC).

When to Seek Medical Help

  • Concerned about your weight or your medicines: talk with your health care provider, CDC says (CDC)
  • Concerned about your eating or your mental health: talk to a primary care provider, who can refer you to a qualified mental health professional, NIMH says (NIMH)
  • A BMI of 30 or more: NIH’s 1998 guidelines say these adults should try to lose weight, and so should adults at 25 to 29.9 (or with a high waist) who have two or more risk factors (NHLBI, 1998)

A provider can also refer you to a registered dietitian or to clinical or community programs, CDC says (CDC). This site does not cover clinical treatment (medicines, procedures or medically supervised diets). For the health risks at different BMI levels, see our BMI and health risks guide.
