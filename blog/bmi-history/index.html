The Complete History of BMI: From Quetelet to Modern Medicine (1832-2026)

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

The Body Mass Index has a 200-year history, from a Belgian mathematician's statistical curiosity to one of the most widely used health metrics in the world. Understanding this history reveals why BMI works the way it does, its original purpose, and why it remains both useful and controversial today.

Adolphe Quetelet: The Father of BMI

The story of BMI begins in mathematics and astronomy. Adolphe Quetelet (1796–1874) was a Belgian mathematician, astronomer and statistician, born in Ghent, who applied probability to human physical characteristics and social aptitudes; he is considered a founder of the social sciences (Eknoyan, 2008).

Quetelet's interest in human measurements arose from his pioneering work in what he called "social physics" — the application of statistical methods to understand human society. He was fascinated by the concept of l'homme moyen, or "the average man," seeking to define the statistical characteristics that defined typical human beings.

"If man increased equally in all dimensions, his weight at different ages would be as the cube of his height. Now, this is not what we really observe." Adolphe Quetelet, A Treatise on Man and the Development of His Faculties (English edition, 1842), as quoted by Wells (2014)

The Birth of the Quetelet Index (1832)

Weight does not grow as the cube of height, as it would if people grew equally in all dimensions. Quetelet’s observation that “the weight increases as the square of the height” became the Quetelet index, described in 1832 (Eknoyan, 2008):

The Quetelet Index (1832)

Weight (kg) ÷ Height² (m)

Quetelet set out his work on the “average man” in his 1835 book Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés (Eknoyan, 2008), published in English in 1842 as A Treatise on Man and the Development of His Faculties (Wells, 2014).

Original Purpose: Populations, Not Individuals

Quetelet wasn’t measuring obesity. In developing his index, Eknoyan writes, his concern was defining the characteristics of “normal man” and fitting the distribution around the norm (Eknoyan, 2008).

Important Historical Context

The search for a practical index of relative weight began after life insurers reported higher death rates among overweight policyholders, and it ended after World War II, when epidemiologists studying heart disease found the Quetelet index the best of them; Keys named it the body mass index in 1972 (Eknoyan, 2008).

Insurance Weight Tables

Metropolitan Life Insurance Tables

Louis Dublin, a statistician and vice president of the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company, led the first tables of normal weights for height; the weights were later split by small, medium and large frames and called “ideal”, then “desirable” (Eknoyan, 2008). The company’s 1959 tables drew on more than 4 million adults, mostly men, insured from 1935 to 1953, and the frame size, judged by elbow width, was never validated (Nuttall, 2015).

Keys and colleagues later preferred the body mass index to percentage of average weight for the simplicity of the calculation and because it applies to all populations at all times (Keys et al., 1972).

Ancel Keys and the Birth of "Body Mass Index" (1972)

The American physiologist Ancel Keys (1904–2004) renamed the Quetelet index (Eknoyan, 2008). In 1972, Keys and his colleagues published “Indices of relative weight and obesity” in the Journal of Chronic Diseases (Keys et al., 1972).

The 1972 Study

Keys and colleagues analysed 7,424 men in 12 cohorts in five countries (the United States, Finland, Italy, South Africa and Japan) and compared these indices (Keys et al., 1972):

The two tests were correlation with height, where lower is better, and with body fatness, measured by skinfolds and, in 180 men, body density, where higher is better (Keys et al., 1972).

"The body mass index seems preferable over other indices of relative weight on these grounds as well as on the simplicity of the calculation and, in contrast to percentage of average weight, the applicability to all populations at all times." Keys et al., Journal of Chronic Diseases (1972)

The paper named the ratio: “the ratio of weight to height squared, here termed the body mass index” (Keys et al., 1972).

What Keys Did and Didn’t Claim

Keys and colleagues judged BMI only slightly better than weight divided by height. And body composition could be measured accurately in large samples only after BMI was already established as the main obesity index (Wells, 2014).

NIH and the 1998 Cut-Offs

Two NIH steps set out how BMI was to be used in the United States.

1985: An NIH Consensus Conference

In February 1985, an NIH consensus conference on the health implications of obesity recommended wider use of BMI, and its summary gave reference tables of BMI values (NIH, 1985). Its panel strongly advised treatment for adults with a BMI above 27.8 for men or 27.3 for women.

1998: The Cut-Offs Used Today

54.9%
US adults overweight or obese (NHANES III)

NIH’s 1998 clinical guidelines set overweight at a BMI of 25 to 29.9 and obesity at 30 or more (NHLBI, 1998). US surveys had counted adults as overweight from a BMI of 27.8 for men and 27.3 for women, so adults between 25 and those values moved into the overweight group (NHLBI, 1998). By the new definitions, 54.9% of US adults were overweight or obese in NHANES III: 32.6% overweight and 22.3% with obesity (NHLBI Evidence Report, 1998).

WHO Global Standardization

WHO’s 1995 report on anthropometry graded adult thinness by BMI: below 18.5, below 17 and below 16 (WHO, 1995). Its 2000 report on obesity set out classes I (30 to 34.9), II (35 to 39.9) and III (40 or more) and concluded that a coherent system based on BMI should be adopted internationally (WHO, 2000).

WHO’s adult categories run from underweight (below 18.5) to class III obesity (40 and above); our BMI categories guide explains each one.

Asian BMI Cutoffs (2004)

A WHO expert consultation kept the international cut-offs but found that the share of Asian people at high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease is substantial below the overweight cut-off of 25, and it named public health action points at a BMI of 23.0, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 (WHO Expert Consultation, 2004).

Modern Critiques and Limitations of BMI

Despite its widespread adoption, BMI has faced growing criticism from researchers, clinicians, and public health advocates. Understanding these limitations is essential for using BMI appropriately. For an in-depth analysis, see our BMI limitations guide.

Core Limitations

1

Doesn't Distinguish Fat from Muscle

BMI treats all weight equally. A muscular athlete and a sedentary person with high body fat can have identical BMI values despite vastly different health profiles.

2

Ignores Fat Distribution

BMI says nothing about where fat sits. NIH’s 1998 guidelines add waist circumference because excess abdominal fat is an independent risk factor for disease (NHLBI, 1998).

3

Height Bias

Standard BMI may underestimate fatness in short people and overestimate it in tall people. This was a key motivation for Trefethen's "New BMI" proposal.

4

Age Not Considered

Body composition changes with age. The same BMI at age 30 and 70 has different implications, yet the formula and cutoffs remain unchanged.

5

Sex Differences Ignored

Women carry more body fat than men at the same BMI. In CDC’s 2011–2018 body scans of adults aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, the median body fat was 33.4% for women and 22.1% for men (our body fat percentage chart uses the same scans).

6

Ethnic Variation

Different ethnic groups have different relationships between BMI and body fat. Standard cutoffs may not apply equally across all populations.

These limitations trace back to BMI’s origin as a population statistic rather than an individual assessment. For athletes and highly muscular individuals, measures of body composition like DEXA scans, hydrostatic weighing, or even simple waist circumference provide more meaningful health information. The "Obesity Paradox"

One pattern called the “obesity paradox” shows up in older adults: compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014).

Proposed Alternatives to BMI

The limitations of BMI have spurred development of alternative metrics. While none has replaced BMI as the global standard, several show promise for specific applications.

Trefethen's "New BMI" (2013)

Oxford mathematician Nick Trefethen proposed a modified formula that uses height to the power of 2.5 instead of 2:

Trefethen's New BMI Formula

New BMI = 1.3 × weight (kg) ÷ height (m)2.5

Trefethen argues that the 2.5 power fits the shapes of short and tall people better than the square, which makes short people think they are thinner, and tall people fatter, than they are; he adds that his formula is not based on epidemiological studies (Trefethen). Despite these alternatives, BMI persists because of its simplicity: it requires only a scale and a measuring tape (or nothing at all if you know your numbers), works across populations, and has decades of research validating its population-level utility.

The Future of Body Composition Metrics

The next decade will likely see significant changes in how we assess body composition and weight-related health risk. Several trends are emerging:

Multi-Metric Approaches

Guidelines pair BMI with other measures. NIH’s 1998 guidelines read waist circumference alongside BMI in adults with a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI, 1998), and NICE now uses waist-to-height ratio (NICE NG246).

Technology-Enabled Assessment

Smart scales that estimate body fat percentage, consumer-grade bioelectrical impedance devices, and even smartphone apps using photos are making body composition assessment more accessible. While not as accurate as clinical methods, these tools can provide more nuanced information than BMI alone.

Precision Medicine

The move toward personalized medicine may eventually lead to individually-calibrated health metrics that account for genetics, ethnicity, age, sex, and medical history.

Will BMI Be Replaced?

Despite its limitations, BMI is unlikely to disappear soon. Its simplicity, universality, and extensive research base make it valuable as an initial screening tool. The future likely involves using BMI as one piece of a larger health assessment puzzle rather than replacing it entirely.
