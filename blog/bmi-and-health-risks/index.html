BMI and Health Risks: Complete Guide to Weight-Related Conditions

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Compared with people at a healthy weight, those with overweight or obesity are at greater risk for many diseases, including diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, stroke and at least 13 types of cancer, and have a higher risk of death from all causes (NCI). NIDDK adds that the risk is greater for people who carry extra fat around the waist (NIDDK). Below: the risk of death across the BMI range, each condition as NIH describes it, and the cases where BMI’s limits make it misjudge risk.

Key Takeaways
  • Risk of death: in pooled data from 239 studies it was lowest at a BMI of 20.0 to 24.9, 1.51 times as high at 15.0 to 18.4 and 2.76 times at 40.0 to 59.9 (GBMC, 2016)
  • Diabetes: nearly 9 in 10 people with type 2 diabetes have overweight or obesity (NIDDK)
  • Blood pressure and stroke: a large body may raise blood pressure because the heart has to pump harder, and high blood pressure is the leading cause of strokes (NIDDK)
  • Cancer: overweight and obesity are linked to 13 cancers, with the largest increases for endometrial and esophageal cancer (NCI)
  • Modest weight loss: in a trial of adults with type 2 diabetes, those who lost 5% to under 10% of their weight in a year were more likely to improve blood sugar, blood pressure and blood fats (Wing et al., 2011).

BMI and the Risk of Death

The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration pooled 239 studies from 32 countries and, to limit the effect of smoking and existing illness, analysed never-smokers without chronic disease at the start, leaving out the first 5 years of follow-up. Against a BMI of 22.5 to 24.9, the risk of death was higher below 20 and at every step above 25 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).

Cardiovascular Disease and BMI

Heart Disease

Having overweight or obesity raises the risk of conditions that can lead to heart disease, such as high blood pressure, high blood cholesterol and high blood glucose, and extra weight can make the heart work harder to send blood to all the cells in the body (NIDDK).

Hypertension (High Blood Pressure)

A large body may raise blood pressure because the heart has to pump harder to supply blood to all the cells, and excess fat may also damage the kidneys, which help regulate blood pressure. High blood pressure can strain the heart, damage blood vessels and raise the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney disease and death (NIDDK).

Stroke

Overweight and obesity are known to raise blood pressure, and high blood pressure is the leading cause of strokes (NIDDK).

Type 2 Diabetes and BMI

Nearly 9 in 10 people with type 2 diabetes have overweight or obesity, and over time high blood glucose can lead to heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, eye problems and nerve damage. For people at risk of type 2 diabetes, NIDDK says losing at least 5% to 7% of their starting weight may prevent or delay it: about 10 to 14 pounds for someone who weighs 200 (NIDDK).

Cancer and BMI

An International Agency for Research on Cancer working group reviewed more than 1,000 studies and found consistent evidence that more body fat goes with a higher risk of a number of cancers. NCI lists 13, and the size of the increase differs between them (NCI):

A study NCI cites estimated that in 2019 about 4.8% of new cancer cases in US men aged 30 and older, and 10.6% in women, were due to excess body weight (NCI).

How Obesity Might Raise Cancer Risk

NCI lists several possible mechanisms:

  • Estrogen: fat tissue makes extra estrogen, and high levels are associated with higher risks of breast, endometrial, ovarian and some other cancers (NCI)
  • Insulin and IGF-1: people with obesity often have higher blood levels of both, and high levels are associated with higher risks of colorectal, thyroid, breast, prostate, ovarian and endometrial cancers (NCI)
  • Inflammation: chronic inflammation, common with obesity, directly promotes tumor growth (NCI)
  • Adipokines: leptin rises with body fat and at high levels can promote abnormal cell growth, while adiponectin, which may hold tumor growth back, is lower in people with obesity (NCI)

Respiratory Conditions

Overweight and obesity can affect how well the lungs work, and excess weight raises the risk of breathing problems (NIDDK).

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

Obesity is a common cause of sleep apnea in adults: with overweight or obesity, more fat may be stored around the neck, which makes the airway smaller. Untreated sleep apnea may raise the risk of many health problems, including heart disease and diabetes, and losing weight may help reduce it or make it go away (NIDDK).

Asthma

Asthma is more likely with obesity, NIDDK says, and so are worse symptoms and a harder time managing it (NIDDK).

Musculoskeletal Problems

Obesity is a leading risk factor for osteoarthritis in the knees, hips and ankles. Extra weight puts more pressure on joints and cartilage, and excess body fat may raise blood levels of substances that cause inflammation, which may raise the risk as well. Gout, arthritis caused by uric acid crystals in the joints, counts obesity among its risk factors too (NIDDK).

Liver Disease (Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease)

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) develop when fat builds up in the liver, which can lead to severe liver damage, cirrhosis or liver failure. They most often affect people with overweight or obesity, and NIDDK says losing at least 3% to 5% of body weight may reduce fat in the liver (NIDDK).

Metabolic syndrome

Metabolic syndrome is a group of conditions that raise the risk of heart disease, diabetes and stroke. A diagnosis needs at least three of these: a large waist, high triglycerides, high blood pressure, high fasting blood glucose and low HDL cholesterol. NIDDK says it is closely linked to overweight and obesity and to a lack of physical activity (NIDDK).

Kidney Disease

Obesity raises the risk of diabetes and high blood pressure, the most common causes of chronic kidney disease (CKD), and even without them obesity may raise the risk of CKD and speed up its progress (NIDDK).

Other Conditions NIDDK Lists

NIDDK also lists (NIDDK):

  • Gallbladder disease, such as gallstones, and inflammation of the pancreas (pancreatitis)
  • Problems in pregnancy, including gestational diabetes, preeclampsia and needing a caesarean delivery
  • Infertility in women and in men
  • Sexual function problems, such as erectile dysfunction

Mental Health Connections

Obesity can also affect mental health, raising the risk of long-term stress, body image problems, low self-esteem, depression and eating disorders. People with overweight or obesity are also likely to face weight-related bias at school and work, which may harm their quality of life over the long term; losing excess weight has been found to improve body image and self-esteem and reduce symptoms of depression (NIDDK). If you have concerns about your eating behavior or mental health, NIMH says to talk to a primary care provider (NIMH).

Health Risks of Being Underweight

Being underweight (BMI below 18.5) carries risks of its own. In the Global BMI Mortality Collaboration’s never-smokers, a BMI of 15.0 to 18.4 carried 1.51 times the risk of death of 22.5 to 24.9, close to the 1.45 of 30.0 to 34.9 (GBMC, 2016).

When BMI may overstate or understate risk

CDC says that for individuals, BMI should be considered with other factors, such as blood pressure, cholesterol levels and a physical examination (CDC).

Older adults (65+)

The Winter et al. 2014 meta-analysis of 197,940 adults aged 65 and over found that, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23, inside the WHO healthy adult range (18.5–24.9), and began to rise at about 33. This is an observed association, not a treatment target.

Fat distribution

Visceral fat, the fat around the internal organs, seems more dangerous for disease risk than overall or subcutaneous fat, which is why waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio and waist-to-height ratio are sometimes used along with BMI (NCI).

“Metabolically healthy” obesity or overweight

In 3.5 million British adults followed for a mean of 5.4 years, people with obesity and none of three metabolic abnormalities (diabetes, high blood pressure, high blood fats) still had a higher risk of coronary heart disease (HR 1.49), cerebrovascular disease (1.07) and heart failure (1.96) than normal-weight people with none (Caleyachetty et al., 2017).

How Reducing BMI Reduces Health Risks

Modest weight loss

In the Look AHEAD trial of adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, those who lost 5% to under 10% of their weight in a year had greater odds of improving blood sugar (HbA1c), blood pressure, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides (Wing et al., 2011).

Reaching and staying at a healthy weight can help prevent these problems, stop them from getting worse or even make them go away, NIDDK says (NIDDK). Our How to lower BMI guide has CDC’s steps; if you’re concerned about your weight, CDC says to talk with your health care provider (CDC).
