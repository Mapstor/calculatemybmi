BMI Chart for Men: Healthy Weight by Height

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

How much should a 5′10″ man weigh? Is 180 pounds too much at that height, or is it muscle? And does the chart change after 60?

Only 24.0% of US men have a BMI in CDC’s healthy range of 18.5 to 24.9, and at every height from 5′4″ to 6′2″ most men are above it (our calculation from CDC measurements, 2021–2023). Since June 2026 the American Diabetes Association also counts waist size: 73.4% of US men meet its new thresholds for obesity, against 39.5% of the same 2,659 men by BMI alone (our calculation).

Below: the chart for every height from 5′2″ to 6′6″ in pounds and kilograms, the 5′10″ answers, and what muscle, waist size and age change.

Key takeaways

A healthy BMI for men is 18.5 to 24.9, the same range as for women (CDC). Under 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 29.9 overweight and 30 or more obesity. At 5′10″, the healthy weight is 129 to 173 lb (59 to 78 kg); at 6′0″, 137 to 183 lb (62 to 83 kg).

  • 24.0% of US men have a healthy BMI and 39.2% have obesity (our calculation from CDC measurements, 2021–2023; NCHS publishes the same obesity rate).
  • Most men with an overweight BMI carry more body fat than the typical man with a healthy BMI. On CDC’s body scans (2011–2018), only 11.8% of men aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 25 to 29.9 carried no more body fat than the median man with a healthy BMI (our calculation).
  • The waist counts too. US guidelines flag a man’s waist over 40 inches, and since June 2026 the American Diabetes Association counts a waist of at least half the height toward obesity from a BMI of 25, or 23 for men of Asian background.
  • The chart is for men 20 and over and doesn’t change with age.

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

How much should a 5′10″ man weigh?

At 5′10″ (177.8 cm), a healthy weight is 129 to 173 lb (59 to 78 kg), a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9. Overweight runs from 174 to 208 lb (79 to 94 kg), and obesity starts at 209 lb (95 kg).

US men of that height are mostly above the range. Of men measured at 5′10″ to the nearest inch, 22.3% have a healthy BMI, 38.3% are overweight and 39.4% have obesity. Their average weight is 205.5 lb and their median 199.1 lb (our calculation from CDC measurements, 313 men, 2021–2023; average weight by height has every height).

Other sources give other numbers for 5′10″. NIH’s printable BMI table starts at a BMI of 19, so its 5′10″ row reads 132 lb under BMI 19 and 167 lb under 24 (NIH); CDC’s healthy range starts at 18.5, which is 129 lb. “Ideal weight” formulas give one weight per height instead of a range; the ideal weight calculator shows four of them. Some online answers put the ideal at 170 to 185 lb, which by BMI runs from 24.4, healthy, to 26.5, overweight.

Example. 185 lb at 5′10″ is 185 × 703 ÷ 70² = 26.5, rounded to one decimal: overweight, 12 lb above the healthy range for that height.

Muscle or fat? Body fat at the same BMI

BMI can’t tell muscle from fat: CDC notes that it “does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass” (CDC). CDC’s survey also measured body fat directly in 2011–2018, with whole-body DXA scans up to age 59, so the two can be compared in the same men.

The median man with a healthy BMI carried 22.1% body fat, with an overweight BMI 26.9% and with obesity 31.6%. Only 11.8% of men with an overweight BMI carried no more body fat than the median man with a healthy BMI, and 1.4% of men with obesity. The mismatch runs the other way too: 14.0% of men with a healthy BMI carried at least as much body fat as the median man with an overweight BMI (our calculation from NHANES 2011–2018 scans).

BMI alone can’t tell which group a man is in. A waist measurement is a quick next check (next section), the body fat calculator estimates body fat from a few measurements, and the body fat percentage chart shows where a reading sits among US men. BMI for athletes covers training.

Waist size and the 2026 ADA standards

US guidelines treat a man’s waist over 40 inches (102 cm) as a sign of higher risk, mainly at a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI). For adults with a BMI under 35, NICE (UK) classes a waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 to 0.59 as increased central adiposity and 0.6 or more as high (NICE).

In June 2026 the American Diabetes Association put the waist into its definition of obesity (ADA). For men of non-Asian background, obesity is a BMI of 30 or more, or a BMI of 25 or more with a waist at least half the height or a waist of 102 cm (40 in) or more. For men of Asian background the lines are a BMI of 27.5, or 23 with the same ratio or a waist of 90 cm (35.5 in) (ADA, 2026). For comparison, CDC’s National Diabetes Prevention Program uses a BMI of 23 or higher, instead of 25, as one of its eligibility criteria for Asian American adults (CDC).

Does the chart change with age?

Not for adults. CDC uses one set of categories for every man aged 20 and over, “regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC). What changes with age is what a given BMI is linked to:

  • Death rates. In 3.95 million never-smokers without certain chronic diseases at the start, leaving out the first five years of follow-up, death rates were lowest at a BMI of 20 to 25. Per 5 BMI units above 25, the risk was 52% higher for BMI measured at ages 35 to 49 and 21% higher at 70 to 89, and the rise was steeper in men than in women (51% against 30%; Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).
  • Older adults. In adults 65 and over, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014). That is an association, not a separate range.
  • Muscle. “Loss of muscle mass in older adults reduces the accuracy of BMI, which may lead to underdiagnosis of obesity” (ADA, 2026).

So a 70-year-old man of 5′9″ has the same healthy range as a 30-year-old: 125 to 168 lb (57 to 76 kg). US men aged 70 to 79 have an average BMI of 29.4 (our calculation, 2021–2023).

Under 20, BMI is read as a percentile for age and sex, not on this chart; the BMI calculator for children and teens works it out for ages 2 to 19. BMI by age explains how the adult range applies across life, and the healthy BMI range guide has the mortality data behind it.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the adult cut-offs and the “regardless of age, sex, or race” rule (page last reviewed March 2024).
  • CDC. Child and Teen BMI Categories: percentiles for ages 2 to 19.
  • CDC. Calculating BMI: the formulas in US units and metric.
  • CDC. About Body Mass Index (BMI): what BMI does and doesn’t measure.
  • NIH (NHLBI). Body Mass Index Table: one column for each whole BMI from 19 to 54.
  • CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 demographics and body measures files (DEMO_L, BMX_L): our calculation of the share of men aged 20 and over in each BMI category by age (2,680 men) and by measured height, of waist size by BMI category (2,596 with a measured waist) and of the 2026 ADA thresholds (2,659 men), weighted with the exam weights; the same files give the average weight at each height on average weight by height.
  • Emmerich SD, et al. NCHS Data Brief No. 508 (Sep 2024): obesity and severe obesity in US adults by sex and age, August 2021–August 2023.
  • CDC NHANES 2011–2018 whole-body DXA scans: our calculation of body fat by BMI category, men aged 20 to 59 (the body fat percentage chart has the method).
  • NHLBI. Clinical Guidelines on the Identification, Evaluation, and Treatment of Overweight and Obesity in Adults, treatment guidelines chapter (NIH, 1998): waist cut-offs for women and men.
  • NICE (UK). Overweight and obesity management, guideline NG246 (2025): waist-to-height ratio categories for adults with a BMI under 35, a 2022 recommendation.
  • American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee for Obesity. Screening, Diagnosis, Evaluation, and Staging of Obesity in Adults: Standards of Care in Overweight and Obesity—2026: obesity thresholds with waist size (recommendations 3.3a and 3.3b); ADA press release, June 2026.
  • CDC. National Diabetes Prevention Program eligibility: BMI of 25 or higher, 23 or higher for Asian American adults.
  • The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration. Body-mass index and all-cause mortality (Lancet, 2016): never-smokers in 189 studies; hazard ratios by age at measurement and by sex.
  • Winter JE, et al. BMI and all-cause mortality in older adults: a meta-analysis (Am J Clin Nutr, 2014).

Method. Men aged 20 and over with a measured height and weight in NHANES August 2021–August 2023, weighted with CDC’s exam weights; heights are measured heights rounded to the nearest inch. Shares carry 95% confidence intervals from Taylor linearization over the survey’s 15 strata, and the page states a difference between age groups only when its interval excludes zero. Body fat comes from whole-body DXA scans of men aged 20 to 59 in NHANES 2011–2018, four survey cycles pooled; men without a valid scan are left out (18.7% of men with a healthy BMI, 18.2% with an overweight BMI and 26.1% with obesity). The ADA comparison leaves out the 21 men whose class depends on a waist that wasn’t measured, and applies the lines for adults of Asian background to the men CDC records as non-Hispanic Asian. The median BMI and the last column of the 5′10″ table read the homepage’s BMI curve for men, as its calculator does; the weight percentiles by height are the ones on average weight by height, and the body fat percentiles the body fat chart’s. Every figure is rounded once, from the unrounded estimate, except that rank, which reads percentile curves stored to two decimals. NHLBI gives the men’s waist line as over 102 cm (over 40 in) and the ADA as 102 cm or more (40 in or more in its Table 3.1); the waist shares on this page use the measured waist in centimeters.
