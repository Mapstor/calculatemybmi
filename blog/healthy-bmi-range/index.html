Healthy BMI Range: What Is a Normal BMI?

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Is a BMI of 23 chubby? What BMI is 70 kg? Does the healthy range change with age, or for women?

Only about 1 in 4 US adults (26.0%) has a BMI in CDC’s healthy range of 18.5 to 24.9. Among 3.95 million never-smokers without chronic disease, the risk of death was lowest at a BMI of 20 to 24.9 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).

Below: healthy weights by height, what death rates show about the range, and US adults in it by age and sex.

Key takeaways

A healthy BMI for adults is 18.5 to 24.9. CDC calls this range a healthy weight and uses it for everyone aged 20 and over, regardless of age, sex or race. A normal BMI means the same range. Under 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight and 30 or more is obesity. Before age 20, BMI is read as a percentile instead.

  • The healthy weight depends only on height: 108 to 145 lb (49 to 65 kg) at 5′4″ and 129 to 173 lb (59 to 78 kg) at 5′10″.
  • 28.1% of US women and 24.0% of men have a healthy BMI; by age, 31.0% at 20 to 39, 22.3% at 40 to 59 and 24.6% at 60 and over (our calculation from CDC measurements, 2021–2023).
  • Risk is not flat inside the range. In never-smokers, the risk of death was 13% higher at 18.5 to 19.9 than at 22.5 to 24.9; just above the range, at 25 to 27.4, it was 7% higher.
  • The range is the same for women and men; the waist cut-off isn’t: 35 inches (88 cm) for women, 40 inches (102 cm) for men.
  • BMI can’t tell muscle from fat, so a waist or body-fat measurement adds what it misses.

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

What is the healthy BMI range?

For adults 20 and older, CDC calls a BMI of 18.5 to less than 25 a healthy weight. BMI is reported to one decimal, so in practice the range is 18.5 to 24.9. CDC bases the adult categories “on a person’s BMI regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC), and WHO’s adult definitions use the same lines: overweight from 25, obesity from 30 (WHO). NIH’s heart institute lists the same ranges (NHLBI). Whether a chart calls it normal, healthy or good, it means this range.

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared, or your weight in pounds times 703 divided by your height in inches squared (CDC). The median US adult has a BMI of 28.2, which is in the overweight range (our calculation from CDC measurements, 2021–2023). Healthy weight by height

In pounds or kilograms, the healthy range depends only on height. At 5′4″ (162.6 cm) it runs from 108 to 145 lb (49 to 65 kg); at 5′10″ (177.8 cm), from 129 to 173 lb (59 to 78 kg). Every 2 inches of height moves the lower limit up by 6 to 8 lb and the upper limit by 8 to 11 lb.

Does the healthy BMI range change with age?

Not for adults. CDC uses one set of categories for everyone aged 20 and over. Three things around it do change with age:

  • Body fat at the same BMI. It depends on age as well as sex (Gallagher et al., 2000), so the same BMI can mean a different amount of fat at 25 and at 75.
  • How strongly BMI tracks death. In never-smokers, per 5 BMI units above 25, the risk of death was 52% higher for BMI measured at ages 35 to 49 but 21% higher for BMI measured at 70 to 89 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).
  • Mortality data in adults 65 and over. In a meta-analysis of 197,940 people aged 65 and over, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014). That is an association, not a separate healthy range or a target.

The BMI calculator by age has more on adults 65 and over, and BMI by age explains how the range applies across adult life.

Children and teens

Under 20, the same BMI means different things at different ages, so CDC reads it as a percentile on growth charts for age and sex. A healthy weight is the 5th to under the 85th percentile; the BMI calculator for children and teens works it out for ages 2 to 19.

Is the healthy BMI range different for women and men?

No. CDC’s cut-offs are the same for women and men, so the healthy weight at a given height is the same too.

Body fat at a given BMI depends on sex (Gallagher et al., 2000): in CDC’s body scans of adults aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, the median woman had 33.4% body fat and the median man 22.1% (our analysis of NHANES 2011–2018). Waist cut-offs also differ by sex: US guidelines treat a waist over 35 inches (88 cm) in women and over 40 inches (102 cm) in men as a marker of higher risk, mainly at a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI).

The link between BMI and death is steeper in men: in never-smokers, per 5 BMI units above 25, the risk of death was 51% higher in men and 30% higher in women (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016). In the US, 28.1% of women and 24.0% of men have a healthy BMI (our calculation from CDC measurements, 2021–2023). The BMI calculators for women and for men add the US data for each sex.

Why 18.5 to 24.9? What the mortality data show

The cut-offs follow health risk. US guidelines link a BMI above the range with higher risk of high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, sleep apnea and osteoarthritis (NHLBI). Those 1998 guidelines already used the 18.5, 25 and 30 cut-offs; later studies tested them against death rates.

The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration pooled 239 studies of 10.6 million people in 32 countries (Lancet, 2016). To keep smoking and existing illness from distorting the picture, its main analysis used 3,951,455 never-smokers who had no chronic disease when they joined and survived the first five years; 385,879 of them died during follow-up.

Crude analyses that ignored smoking and existing disease gave exaggerated risks for underweight and inverted ones for overweight, so overweight looked protective. The authors could reproduce a 2013 review’s finding that overweight carried a lower risk of death in analyses with inadequate control for these biases, but not in the strict one.

The risk of death was lowest at a BMI of 20 to 24.9. Compared with 22.5 to 24.9, it was 13% higher at 18.5 to 19.9 and 51% higher at 15 to 18.4. Above the range it rose at every step: 7% higher at 25 to 27.4, 20% at 27.5 to 29.9, 45% at 30 to 34.9, 94% at 35 to 39.9, and 2.76 times as high at 40 to 59.9.

Per 5 BMI units above 25, the risk of death in never-smokers was 39% higher in Europe and in East Asia, 31% in Australia and New Zealand and 29% in North America; the authors write that “the associations of both overweight and obesity with higher all-cause mortality were broadly consistent in four continents”. These are associations in observational data; they don’t show how much changing your own BMI would change your risk.

Does the healthy BMI range change with ethnicity?

In the US, CDC’s BMI categories don’t change with race, but its National Diabetes Prevention Program takes Asian American adults from a BMI of 23 instead of 25 (CDC). Outside the US, three documents address it:

  • The WHO Western Pacific Region, the International Association for the Study of Obesity and the International Obesity Task Force proposed lower cut-offs for Asian adults in 2000: overweight from 23 and obesity from 25 (cited in Ko and Tang, 2006).
  • A WHO expert consultation in 2004 found that the share of Asian people at high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease is substantial below a BMI of 25. It kept the international categories for everyone and added BMI 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 as points for public-health action (WHO, 2004).
  • In the UK, NICE says people with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background are prone to central adiposity and reach cardiometabolic risk at a lower BMI. It uses overweight from 23 to 27.4 and obesity from 27.5 for them (NICE guideline NG246, a 2022 recommendation).

Above 25, the link between BMI and death was as strong in East Asia as in Europe: in never-smokers, per 5 BMI units, the risk of death was 39% higher in both (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016). The study had too few deaths in South Asia to give a reliable estimate there.

What a healthy BMI can’t tell you

CDC calls BMI “a quick, safe, and reliable screening measure” but notes that it “does not directly measure body fat” and “does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass” (CDC). For one person, CDC says BMI “should be considered with other factors”. Three checks fill the gaps:

  • Waist. Waist size reflects fat around the abdomen, which US guidelines treat as an independent predictor of risk (NHLBI); the waist-to-height ratio compares it with your height.
  • Body fat. The body fat calculator estimates it from tape or skinfold measurements, and the body fat percentage chart shows typical values from CDC body scans.
  • Muscle. Because BMI can’t separate muscle from fat, a muscular person can have a BMI over 25 with little body fat; BMI for athletes goes further.

In 2025 the Lancet Commission on Clinical Obesity recommended confirming excess body fat with a body measurement such as waist size, or with a direct body-fat measurement, instead of diagnosing obesity from BMI alone; above a BMI of 40 it says excess fat can be assumed. BMI limitations covers the rest.

How to reach or stay in the healthy range

Official US guidance, and what one large trial found:

  • Activity. “[A]dults need at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity” a week, plus muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days (Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans).
  • A plan for a goal weight. NIH’s Body Weight Planner builds a calorie and physical activity plan for a goal weight, for adults who aren’t pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Above 25. In an analysis of a large trial (Look AHEAD) in adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, losing 5 to 10% of body weight was linked to better blood sugar, blood pressure, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides after one year (Wing et al., 2011). How to lower your BMI covers diet, activity and when to seek medical help.
  • Under 18.5. CDC notes that “poor nutrition and underlying health conditions can result in underweight” (Health E-Stat 119). MedlinePlus (NIH) advises contacting a health care provider if you lose more than 10 lb (4.5 kg) or 5% of your body weight within 6 to 12 months without knowing why. Underweight BMI risks covers the causes.

Method. Shares of US adults: CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023, adults aged 20 and over with a measured height and weight, pregnant women excluded, weighted with the exam weights; BMI as CDC records it, to one decimal; the shares reproduce NCHS’s published rates (listed in the note under the US adults table). Hazard ratios are quoted from the Global BMI Mortality Collaboration as published; percentages such as “13% higher” are the hazard ratio minus 1.
