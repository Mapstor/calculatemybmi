BMI Categories: All Weight Classifications Explained

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

The World Health Organization divides adult BMI into eight categories, from severe thinness to class III obesity. Below: each category’s range, what large studies tie to it, healthy weights by height, and where the categories fall short.

Key Takeaways
  • Eight WHO categories: Severe thinness, moderate thinness, mild thinness, normal, overweight, obese I, obese II, obese III
  • Normal BMI range: 18.5 – 24.9, the CDC and WHO healthy-weight category
  • Risk at both ends: in pooled data from 239 studies, the risk of death was lowest at a BMI of 20.0 to 24.9 and higher below and above it (GBMC, 2016)
  • A screening measure, not a diagnosis: for individuals, CDC says BMI should be read with other factors, such as blood pressure and cholesterol (CDC)
  • Use our free BMI calculator to find your category instantly

The Complete BMI Classification System

The World Health Organization graded adult thinness in a 1995 expert report and set out the obesity classes in a 2000 technical report; CDC uses the same cut-offs from 18.5 up for adults aged 20 and older, with a single underweight category below 18.5:

Children and teens aged 2 through 19 are classed differently: because they are growing, CDC compares their BMI with others of the same sex and age, as BMI-for-age percentiles (CDC). See our Pediatric BMI Calculator for the child and teen categories.

Category 1: Severe Thinness / Grade 3 Underweight (BMI Below 16.0)

WHO calls a BMI below 16.0 severe thinness: grade 3, the lowest of its three underweight grades (WHO, 1995). Our underweight BMI risks guide covers the risks and causes in detail.

NIMH lists extreme thinness among the signs of anorexia nervosa, and says people with any combination of its signs should talk to a health care provider (NIMH).

Category 2: Moderate Thinness / Grade 2 Underweight (BMI 16.0–16.9)

WHO calls a BMI of 16.0 to 16.9 moderate thinness, grade 2 (WHO, 1995).

Category 3: Mild Thinness / Grade 1 Underweight (BMI 17.0–18.4)

WHO calls a BMI of 17.0 to 18.4 mild thinness, grade 1 (WHO, 1995). CDC does not split the grades: it puts every BMI below 18.5 in one category, underweight (CDC).

Why Some Adults Are Underweight

CDC’s survey team names two broad causes: “Poor nutrition and underlying health conditions can result in underweight among youth and adults” (NCHS). For weight lost without trying, MedlinePlus lists a wider set, from depression and long-term infections to problems absorbing food, such as celiac disease (MedlinePlus).

Risk data come in wider bands than WHO’s grades: in pooled data from 239 studies, the risk of death at a BMI of 15.0 to 18.4 was higher than at 22.5 to 24.9 (hazard ratio 1.51; GBMC, 2016).

MedlinePlus advises seeing a provider if you lose more than 10 pounds (4.5 kg), or 5% of your usual weight, within 6 to 12 months without knowing why (MedlinePlus). If you have concerns about your eating behavior or mental health, NIMH says to talk to a primary care provider (NIMH).

Category 4: Normal Weight (BMI 18.5–24.9)

CDC calls a BMI from 18.5 to just under 25 healthy weight (CDC). That is a span of about 6.5 BMI points, a sizeable weight difference at any height. For more context, see our guide to the healthy BMI range.

Optimal BMI Within Normal Range

CDC draws no line inside the range (CDC). In the same pooled data from 239 studies, the lowest risk of death sat at a BMI of 20.0 to 24.9; at 18.5 to 19.9 it was a little higher (hazard ratio 1.13 against 22.5 to 24.9; GBMC, 2016). Category 5: Overweight / Pre-obese (BMI 25.0–29.9)

The overweight category encompasses a BMI of 25.0 to 29.9. WHO’s name for it is pre-obese (WHO, 2000). In 2021–2023, 31.7% of US adults were in this range (age-adjusted; NCHS Health E-Stat 111). Learn more about the health implications in our BMI and health risks guide.

NCI says that, compared with people at a healthy weight, those with overweight or obesity are at greater risk for many diseases, including diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, stroke and at least 13 types of cancer (NCI).

The Overweight Debate

Two large pooled analyses disagree about this range. In one, of 97 studies, all-cause mortality in the overweight range was slightly lower than in the healthy range (hazard ratio 0.94; Flegal et al., JAMA 2013). In the other, of 239 studies, whose main results kept to never-smokers without known disease and left out the first five years of follow-up, the risk was higher: hazard ratios 1.07 at a BMI of 25.0 to 27.4 and 1.20 at 27.5 to 29.9, against 22.5 to 24.9 (GBMC, 2016).

For a deeper exploration, see our BMI Calculator by Age guide.

Category 6: Obese Class I (BMI 30.0–34.9)

The obesity-class sections below give the WHO classification and the risks reported at each class. They are not personal medical advice, and this site does not cover treatment: medicines, surgery or other care.

WHO calls a BMI of 30.0 to 34.9 obese class I; CDC calls it class 1 obesity (CDC). In the 239-study data, the risk of death at 30.0 to 34.9 was higher than at 22.5 to 24.9 (hazard ratio 1.45; GBMC, 2016); the 2013 analysis of 97 studies found no clear difference at this grade (hazard ratio 0.95; Flegal et al., JAMA 2013). For the health-risk discussion see our BMI and health risks guide.

Category 7: Obese Class II (BMI 35.0–39.9)

WHO calls a BMI of 35.0 to 39.9 obese class II; CDC calls it class 2 obesity (CDC). The risk of death at 35.0 to 39.9 was higher again (hazard ratio 1.94 against 22.5 to 24.9; GBMC, 2016).

In a pooled analysis of 97 studies covering more than 2.88 million people, all-cause mortality was higher at a BMI of 35 or more than in the healthy range (hazard ratio 1.29; Flegal et al., JAMA 2013).

Category 8: Obese Class III (BMI 40.0 or Higher)

WHO calls a BMI of 40.0 or more obese class III; CDC calls it class 3 obesity, NCHS reports it as severe obesity, and it is sometimes called morbid obesity. In US adults in 2021–2023, 9.7% had severe obesity (age-adjusted; NCHS). At a BMI of 40.0 to 59.9, the risk of death was the highest of any band in the 239-study data (hazard ratio 2.76 against 22.5 to 24.9; GBMC, 2016).

The higher all-cause mortality reported for BMI 35 and above in the pooled analysis (hazard ratio 1.29) includes this class (Flegal et al., JAMA 2013).

Lower action points for Asian populations

In 2004, a WHO expert consultation found that a substantial share of Asian people have a high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at BMIs below 25. It identified BMI 23 and 27.5 as additional public-health action points (increased and high risk), while keeping the international categories. In the UK, NICE uses lower thresholds for adults with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background: overweight from 23 to 27.4 and obesity from 27.5 (NICE guideline NG246).

Why 25 and 30? The History Behind the Cutoffs

The current BMI cutoff values did not emerge from a single study. They evolved over decades:

  • Before 1998: US surveys counted adults as overweight at a BMI of 27.8 or more for men and 27.3 or more for women, the 85th percentiles at ages 20 to 29 in NHANES II (NHLBI, 1998).
  • 1995: WHO graded adult thinness by BMI: below 18.5, below 17 and below 16 (WHO, 1995).
  • 1998: NIH’s clinical guidelines set overweight at a BMI of 25 to 29.9 and obesity at 30 or more. Nobody’s weight changed, but adults between 25 and the old thresholds now counted as overweight (NHLBI, 1998).
  • 2000: WHO’s report on obesity (Technical Report 894) set out obesity classes I, II and III and concluded that a coherent system based on BMI should be adopted internationally.

Risk does not jump at either cut-off: in the 239-study data, the risk of death rose band by band through 25 and 30 (GBMC, 2016). Learn more about the formula behind these numbers in our BMI Formula guide.

Category Borderlines and What They Mean

Near a boundary, a small change in weight can move you into the next category. What each boundary means:

BMI 18–19: near the underweight / normal boundary

  • Just below 18.5 is CDC’s underweight and WHO’s mild thinness; from 18.5 it is CDC’s healthy weight and WHO’s normal range (WHO, 1995; CDC).
  • Losing weight without trying: MedlinePlus’s rule on when to get checked is under Why Some Adults Are Underweight.

BMI 24–26: near the normal / overweight boundary

  • 25.0 is where CDC’s overweight category and WHO’s pre-obese range start (CDC; WHO, 2000).
  • From a BMI of 25, NIH guidelines add a waist measurement (see Measure Your Waist below).

BMI 29–31: near the overweight / obese boundary

  • 30.0 is where class 1 obesity starts for CDC and obese class I for WHO (CDC; WHO, 2000).
  • In a large trial of adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, losing 5–10% of body weight was linked to improvements in blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol within a year (Wing et al., 2011).

Limitations of BMI Categories

CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure,” but notes that it does not directly measure body fat (CDC). For more, see our BMI limitations guide.

  • Fat, muscle and bone: CDC notes that BMI does not distinguish between them (CDC). The lean body mass calculator estimates the part of your weight that isn’t fat.
  • Where fat sits: NIH guidelines call excess abdominal fat “an important, independent risk factor for disease” and pair BMI with a waist measurement (NHLBI).
  • One set of cut-offs: CDC applies the adult categories to everyone 20 and older regardless of age, sex or race (CDC), yet NCHS notes that at a given BMI, body fat may vary by sex, age, and race and Hispanic origin (NCHS).
  • Ethnicity: NCHS notes that health risks may begin at a lower BMI among Asian people, and that BMI may overestimate body fat among Black people at a given BMI compared with others (NCHS).

Tips for Understanding Your BMI Category

1

Look Beyond the Label

A category is a screening result, not a diagnosis: for individuals, CDC says BMI should be considered with other factors, such as blood pressure, cholesterol levels and a physical examination (CDC).

2

Measure Your Waist

NIH guidelines pair BMI with a waist measurement: more than 40 inches (102 cm) for men or 35 inches (88 cm) for women marks higher risk, at a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI).

3

Account for Age and Sex

The adult categories don’t change with age or sex, but our BMI by age guide and the calculators for women and men show how a result compares with US adults of the same age or sex.

4

If Your Weight Concerns You

If you’re concerned about your weight or have questions about your medications, CDC says to talk with your health care provider (CDC).
