BMI Categories: All Weight Classifications Explained

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Is a BMI of 29.9 obese? Are obese and morbidly obese different? Is 300 lb morbidly obese?

Most US adults sit above the healthy range: 32.1% are overweight and 40.3% have obesity, 9.4% in class 3 (our calculation from CDC measurements, 2021–2023).

Below: the six categories and three obesity classes, how many US adults fall in each by age, the lower lines for Asian adults, and the history behind 25 and 30.

Key takeaways

Adults have six BMI categories: underweight (under 18.5), healthy weight (18.5 to 24.9), overweight (25 to 29.9) and obesity in three classes, class 1 (30 to 34.9), class 2 (35 to 39.9) and class 3 (40 or more). CDC uses the same lines for women and men aged 20 and over; children use percentiles.

  • 40.3% of US adults have obesity: 20.6% class 1, 10.3% class 2 and 9.4% class 3. Another 32.1% are overweight and 26.0% at a healthy weight (2021–2023, our calculation, matching NCHS’s published rates).
  • Class 3 obesity is more common in women: 12.1% against 6.7% of men (NCHS).
  • The overweight line moved in 1998. US surveys had counted adults as overweight from 27.8 (men) or 27.3 (women); NIH set 25 for both. 17.5% of US adults today have a BMI between 25 and the old lines (our calculation).
  • Lower lines for Asian adults. WHO’s 2004 action points are 23 and 27.5. Among non-Hispanic Asian US adults, 27.6% have a BMI of 27.5 or more, about twice the 13.4% at 30 or more (our calculation).
  • BMI can’t tell muscle from fat (CDC), so a waist or body-fat measurement adds what it misses.

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for information only. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

The six adult BMI categories

For adults 20 and older, CDC uses four categories and splits the fourth, obesity, into three classes (CDC). NIH’s 1998 guidelines set the same lines (NHLBI, 1998), and WHO’s report on obesity uses them too, calling the classes obese class I, II and III (WHO, 2000).

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared, or your weight in pounds times 703 divided by your height in inches squared (CDC). It is reported to one decimal, so 24.9 is the top of the healthy range and 25.0 the start of overweight. The BMI chart gives the weight for every category at each height, and the BMI calculator works out yours.

The three classes of obesity

CDC splits obesity, every BMI of 30 or more, into three classes: class 1 is 30 to 34.9, class 2 is 35 to 39.9 and class 3 is 40 or more (CDC). NCHS reports class 3 as severe obesity (NCHS), and it is sometimes called morbid obesity. In pounds, each class starts at a different weight for each height:

In never-smokers without chronic disease, the risk of death rose with each class. Against a BMI of 22.5 to 24.9, it was 1.45 times as high at 30 to 34.9, 1.94 times at 35 to 39.9 and 2.76 times at 40 to 59.9 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).

Class 3 obesity is more common in women than in men: 12.1% against 6.7% (NCHS Data Brief 508). So is class 2 (11.5% against 9.0%), while class 1 is more common in men (23.6% against 17.7%) (our calculation; both differences hold at 95% confidence).

Underweight and WHO’s thinness grades

Underweight is a BMI under 18.5 (CDC), and 1.6% of US adults were underweight in 2021–2023 (Health E-Stat 119). WHO’s 1995 report on anthropometry grades it further (WHO, 1995):

GradeBMI
Grade 1, mild thinness17.0–18.4

CDC notes that “poor nutrition and underlying health conditions can result in underweight” (Health E-Stat 119). Underweight BMI risks covers the causes and when to get help.

The same lines for women, men and every adult age

CDC bases the adult categories on BMI “regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC), so a woman and a man of the same height cross each line at the same weight. What changes with age is how many adults sit in each category:

A healthy BMI is more common at 20 to 39 (31.0%) than at 40 to 59 (22.3%). Class 1 obesity is more common at 60 and over (22.3%) than at 20 to 39 (16.5%), while class 3 is less common at 60 and over (6.6%) than at 40 to 59 (12.0%). The page states a difference only when its 95% confidence interval excludes zero.

For adults 65 and over, a meta-analysis found a higher risk of death below a BMI of 23 (Winter et al., 2014), but the categories themselves don’t change with age. BMI by age covers it.

Children and teens: percentiles, not fixed lines

From 2 to 19, BMI is compared with children of the same sex and age, as a BMI-for-age percentile (CDC). The BMI calculator for children and teens works it out.

Lower action points for Asian adults

In 2004, a WHO expert consultation found that the share of Asian people at high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease is substantial at BMIs below 25. It kept the international categories and added 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 as points for public-health action (WHO, 2004). Two guidelines use the lower lines:

  • CDC’s National Diabetes Prevention Program takes Asian American adults from a BMI of 23 instead of 25 (CDC).
  • In the UK, NICE uses overweight from 23 to 27.4 and obesity from 27.5 for people with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background (NICE guideline NG246).

Among non-Hispanic Asian US adults measured in 2021–2023, 13.4% had a BMI of 30 or more, and 27.6% had 27.5 or more. At the overweight lines, 45.2% had 25 or more and 65.5% had 23 or more (our calculation; 95% confidence intervals in the method note).

Where 25 and 30 came from

  • Before 1998: US surveys counted adults as overweight from a BMI of 27.8 for men and 27.3 for women, the 85th percentiles at ages 20 to 29 in NHANES II (NHLBI, 1998).
  • 1995: WHO graded adult thinness at BMI 18.5, 17 and 16 (WHO, 1995).
  • 1998: NIH’s clinical guidelines set overweight at 25 to 29.9 and obesity at 30 or more (NHLBI, 1998).
  • 2000: WHO’s report on obesity set out obese classes I, II and III and concluded that a coherent system based on BMI “should be adopted internationally” (WHO, 2000).

Nobody’s weight changed in 1998, but the line did. Measured today, 17.5% of US adults have a BMI from 25 up to the old lines: overweight now, not before 1998. That is 21.6% of men and 13.7% of women; the men’s old line sat higher, at 27.8. Under the old lines, 54.8% of adults would count as overweight or above; under today’s, 72.4% do (our calculation).

In the 239-study analysis, the risk of death in never-smokers climbed band by band from 25 upward: 1.07 times the reference at 25 to 27.4, 1.20 at 27.5 to 29.9 and 1.45 at 30 to 34.9 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).

Close to a line

Because BMI is reported to one decimal, a category can change with a pound or two. At 5′10″, a BMI of 29.9 and a BMI of 30.0 are 0.7 lb apart, and each whole BMI unit is 7.0 lb.

What a category can’t tell you

CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure” but notes that it “does not directly measure body fat” and “does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass” (CDC). For one person, CDC says BMI “should be considered with other factors”. A muscular person can reach the overweight range with little body fat; the waist-to-height ratio and the body fat calculator measure what BMI misses, and BMI limitations covers the rest.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the adult categories, the three obesity classes and the “regardless of age, sex, or race” rule.
  • CDC. Child and Teen BMI Categories: percentiles for ages 2 to 19.
  • CDC. About Body Mass Index (BMI): what BMI measures and what it misses.
  • CDC. Calculating BMI: the formulas in metric and US units.
  • NHLBI. Clinical Guidelines on the Identification, Evaluation, and Treatment of Overweight and Obesity in Adults (NIH, 1998): the categories and the pre-1998 survey lines.
  • WHO. Physical status: the use and interpretation of anthropometry (Technical Report Series 854, 1995): thinness grades.
  • WHO. Obesity: preventing and managing the global epidemic (Technical Report Series 894, 2000): obese classes I, II and III.
  • WHO Expert Consultation. The Lancet, 2004: BMI in Asian populations and the public-health action points.
  • NICE (UK). Overweight and obesity management, guideline NG246: lower BMI thresholds for people from some ethnic backgrounds (recommendation 1.9.11, 2022).
  • CDC. National Diabetes Prevention Program eligibility: BMI of 25 or higher, 23 or higher for Asian American adults.
  • The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration. Body-mass index and all-cause mortality (Lancet, 2016): hazard ratios by BMI band in never-smokers without chronic disease.
  • Winter JE, et al. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2014: BMI and mortality in adults 65 and over.
  • Emmerich SD, et al. NCHS Data Brief No. 508 (Sep 2024): obesity and severe obesity in US adults, August 2021–August 2023.
  • Fryar CD, Afful J, Saif NT. NCHS Health E-Stat 111: overweight and obesity in US adults.
  • NCHS. Health E-Stat 119: underweight in US adults.
  • CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 exam files (DEMO_L, BMX_L): our calculation of the share of adults aged 20 and over in each category by age and sex (5,929: 2,680 men, 3,249 women), the 1998 line move and the Asian action points (330 non-Hispanic Asian adults).

Method. US adults: CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023, adults aged 20 and over with a measured height and weight, pregnant women excluded, weighted with the exam weights; crude shares, BMI as CDC records it, to one decimal. The shares reproduce NCHS’s published crude rates: obesity 40.3% (39.2% of men, 41.3% of women), class 3 9.4%, overweight 32.1% and underweight 1.6%. 95% confidence intervals by Taylor linearisation (15 design degrees of freedom); a difference is stated only when its interval excludes zero. The 1998 move counts men with a BMI of 25.0 to 27.7 and women with 25.0 to 27.2. Non-Hispanic Asian adults (330): BMI 30 or more 13.4% (95% CI 6.3–20.4%), 27.5 or more 27.6% (19.9–35.3%), 25 or more 45.2% (35.5–55.0%), 23 or more 65.5% (56.8–74.2%). Weights where each obesity class starts: the lightest whole pound and whole kilogram whose BMI, rounded to one decimal, is in the class.
