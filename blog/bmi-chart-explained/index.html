How to Read a BMI Chart

Looking for the full chart? See the BMI chart for adults for the complete height-by-weight table (imperial and metric).

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

A BMI chart is a visual reference table that shows Body Mass Index values for different height and weight combinations. Instead of calculating BMI with a formula, you simply find where your height row intersects with your weight column to instantly see your BMI and weight category. Below: how to read the chart, what its colours mean, and when a BMI calculator is the better tool.

Key Takeaways
  • BMI charts show BMI values at the intersection of height (rows) and weight (columns)
  • Color coding: Blue = underweight, Green = normal (18.5-24.9), Yellow/Orange = overweight, Red = obese
  • Standard charts use the WHO categories, which CDC applies to adults 20 and older regardless of sex
  • Finding your BMI: Locate height on left, move right to weight, read BMI at intersection
  • Charts vs calculators: Both give the same result; charts show context, calculators give precision
  • Limitations: Charts cannot account for muscle mass, age, ethnicity, or fat distribution

What Is a BMI Chart?

A BMI chart (also called a BMI table, BMI grid, or height-weight chart) is a pre-calculated reference table that displays BMI values for various height and weight combinations. Rather than using the BMI formula (weight in kg divided by height in meters squared), you can look up your BMI directly by finding the intersection of your height and weight on the chart.

Compared with working out the formula, a chart gives you:

  • Instant visual lookup: No math required to find your BMI
  • Context at a glance: See how your BMI relates to surrounding values
  • Category identification: Color coding shows whether you fall in underweight, normal, overweight, or obese ranges
  • Weight range awareness: See how much weight change would move you between categories

NHLBI publishes a BMI table for adults (NHLBI), and CDC and WHO define the adult categories its colours follow (CDC; WHO).

How to Read a BMI Chart

Reading a BMI chart is straightforward once you understand the structure. Here is the step-by-step process:

Find Your Height

Locate your height in the leftmost column (vertical axis). Charts may list height in feet and inches, inches only, centimeters, or meters depending on whether they use imperial or metric units.

Find Your Weight

Look along the top row (horizontal axis) to find your weight. This will be in pounds or kilograms. If your exact weight is not listed, use the nearest value or interpolate between two columns.

Find the Intersection

Move horizontally from your height row and vertically from your weight column until they meet. The number at this intersection is your BMI.

Identify Your Category

Note the color of the cell or check the BMI value against the category ranges: under 18.5 is underweight, 18.5-24.9 is normal, 25-29.9 is overweight, and 30+ is obese.

Example

You are 5′6″ (167.6 cm) and weigh 160 lb (72.6 kg). In the chart above, the 5′6″ row meets the 160 lb column at 26, in the overweight color. The exact BMI is 25.8 (160 × 703 ÷ 66²), inside the overweight range of 25.0 to 29.9.

BMI Charts for Men vs Women

Standard BMI charts use identical categories for both men and women. The WHO, CDC, and NIH all apply the same thresholds regardless of sex: 18.5 for underweight, 25 for overweight, and 30 for obesity. However, this one-size-fits-all approach has limitations.

Why the Same Categories Apply to Both Sexes

WHO’s 2000 report concluded that a coherent system of BMI classes should be adopted internationally (WHO, 2000), and CDC applies its categories regardless of sex (CDC). Body fat differs at the same BMI: in CDC’s 2011–2018 body scans of adults aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, the median body fat was 33.4% for women and 22.1% for men (our body fat percentage chart uses the same scans).

For sex-specific guidance, see our detailed guides on BMI charts for women and BMI charts for men. You can also use our specialized calculators: Women's BMI Calculator and Men's BMI Calculator.

Age and BMI charts

Standard BMI charts apply the same WHO categories to all adults regardless of age. WHO does not publish separate age-adjusted cut-offs, and neither does the CDC. In adults 65 and over, a meta-analysis found that, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014).

Why age matters for BMI interpretation

  • Body fat at the same BMI: rises with age; at a BMI of 25, Gallagher and colleagues predicted 20% body fat for African American and white men aged 20 to 39 and 25% at 60 to 79 (Gallagher et al., 2000, Table 4).
  • Caution after 65: NICE advises interpreting BMI with caution in people aged 65 and over, taking into account other conditions and the possible protective effect of a slightly higher BMI when older (NICE NG246).

Observational reference (older adults only)

How to Find Your BMI on a Chart Without Calculating

One of the main advantages of BMI charts is finding your BMI without doing any math. Here is a detailed walkthrough:

1

Know Your Measurements

You need your height and weight in the same unit system the chart uses. Measure height without shoes and weight with minimal clothing, at a consistent time of day.

2

Match the Chart Format

Use an imperial chart (feet/inches, pounds) or metric chart (cm, kg) based on how you know your measurements. Do not mix units.

3

Find Nearest Values

If your exact height or weight is not listed, use the closest value or estimate between two rows/columns. For example, if you are 5'7" and the chart shows 5'6" and 5'8", your BMI will be between those two rows.

4

Trace to the Intersection

Use a straightedge (ruler, piece of paper, or your finger) to trace from your height row horizontally and from your weight column vertically until they meet.

5

Read the BMI Value

The number at the intersection is your BMI. If it falls between listed values, estimate accordingly. Most charts round to whole numbers.

6

Check the Category

Note the cell color or check the BMI value against the ranges: under 18.5 (underweight), 18.5-24.9 (normal), 25-29.9 (overweight), 30+ (obese).

Quick Reference

For normal weight at any height: Look for where the green zone begins (around the 18.5 BMI line) and ends (around the 25 BMI line). Any weight in this green band is considered normal for that height.

Interpreting BMI Chart Zones

Each colour on the chart is one of CDC’s adult categories. Here is what the sources say about each:

Underweight Zone (Blue - BMI Under 18.5)

A BMI below 18.5 is underweight (CDC). If you are losing weight without trying, MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider after a loss of about 10 pounds (4.5 kg), or 5% of your normal weight, over 6 to 12 months or less without a known reason (MedlinePlus). Our guide to underweight BMI risks covers the rest.

Normal Weight Zone (Green - BMI 18.5-24.9)

CDC calls a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 healthy weight (CDC); our healthy BMI range guide goes further. In a pooled analysis of 239 studies, all-cause mortality was lowest at a BMI of 20 to 25 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).

Overweight Zone (Yellow/Orange - BMI 25-29.9)

A BMI of 25 to 29.9 is overweight (CDC). BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle and bone mass, so a muscular person can fall in this range, and for individuals CDC says BMI should be considered with other factors, such as blood pressure and cholesterol levels (CDC). NIH reads the waist alongside BMI from 25 to 34.9: over 40 in (102 cm) for men or 35 in (88 cm) for women marks higher risk (NHLBI, 1998).

Obese Zone (Red - BMI 30+)

A BMI of 30 or more is obesity, in three classes: 30 to 34.9, 35 to 39.9 and 40 or more (WHO, 2000). People with obesity are at greater risk for diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, stroke and at least 13 types of cancer (NCI). In the Look AHEAD trial of adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes, those who lost 5 to 10% of their weight in a year had greater odds of better blood sugar, blood pressure, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides (Wing et al., 2011). If you’re concerned about your weight, CDC says to talk with your health care provider (CDC).

Limitations of BMI Charts

CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure”, but it does not directly measure body fat (CDC).

What BMI Charts Do Well

  • Quick, low-cost screening
  • The same categories for every adult
  • No arithmetic: you look up a cell

What BMI Charts Cannot Do

  • Tell fat from muscle and bone
  • Measure body fat
  • Show where fat sits
  • Allow for age: body fat at the same BMI rises with age
  • Allow for family background

Sources: CDC, About BMI; CDC, adult categories; NIH reads the waist alongside BMI (NHLBI, 1998); Gallagher et al., 2000; lower thresholds for some family backgrounds (NICE NG246).

Who May Be Misclassified by BMI Charts

BMI can misread these groups:

  • Athletes and muscular individuals: BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle and bone mass (CDC), so a very muscular person can reach an overweight or obese BMI; NICE says its waist-to-height ratio classes can be used in adults with high muscle mass (NICE NG246)
  • Older adults: body fat at the same BMI rises with age (Gallagher et al., 2000), and NICE advises caution in reading BMI at 65 and over (NICE NG246)
  • People with a lot of fat around the waist: in CDC’s 2021–2023 survey, 50.2% of women and 42.9% of men with a healthy BMI had a waist of at least half their height (our calculation from CDC NHANES 2021–2023); the waist-to-height ratio calculator checks yours
  • Asian populations: a WHO expert consultation found the share of Asian people at high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease substantial below a BMI of 25, and named lower action points (Lancet, 2004)
  • Black African or African-Caribbean family background: NICE uses lower thresholds, overweight from 23 and obesity from 27.5, as for South Asian, Chinese, other Asian and Middle Eastern backgrounds (NICE NG246)

For more on what BMI misses, see our comprehensive BMI Limitations guide.
