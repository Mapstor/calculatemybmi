BMI Limitations: 10 Critical Reasons Why BMI May Be Misleading You

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

CDC calls BMI “a quick, low-cost, and reliable screening measure” for underweight, overweight or obesity, but notes that it does not directly measure body fat (CDC). Below: ten ways a BMI can mislead, what the sources say about each, and the measures that fill the gaps.

Key Takeaways
  • BMI treats fat, muscle and bone alike: CDC notes it does not distinguish between them (CDC)
  • Where fat sits matters: NIH guidelines call excess abdominal fat an independent risk factor and add a waist measurement (NHLBI)
  • Same BMI, different body fat: at a given BMI, body fat may vary by sex, age, and race and Hispanic origin, NCHS notes (NCHS)
  • A healthy BMI can still mean high body fat (“normal weight obesity”): BMI does not measure body fat directly (CDC)
  • Measures that fill the gaps: a waist measurement, the waist-to-height ratio, a body fat estimate or a DXA scan (see the table below)
  • Use our BMI calculator as a starting point, but consider additional metrics for a complete picture

What Is BMI and Why Was It Created?

Body Mass Index began as the Quetelet index, described in 1832 by the Belgian mathematician, astronomer and statistician Adolphe Quetelet (Eknoyan, 2008). He was studying the “average man”, and in developing the index he had no interest in obesity (Eknoyan, 2008).

Ancel Keys named it the Body Mass Index in 1972 (Eknoyan, 2008); our BMI history follows how health agencies took it up. It needs only two measurements: height and weight.

Limitation #1: BMI Cannot Distinguish Muscle from Fat

BMI is weight divided by height squared, so a kilogram of muscle counts the same as a kilogram of fat. CDC notes that BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle and bone mass (CDC). The spread is wide even inside the healthy range: on CDC’s DXA scans of adults aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, the middle half of women measured 29.8% to 36.5% body fat and the middle half of men 18.6% to 25.0% (our calculation from CDC NHANES 2011–2018 DXA scans).

Limitation #2: It Ignores Fat Distribution

BMI uses total weight, so it cannot show where fat is stored. US clinical guidelines treat fat around the waist as a risk marker in its own right and pair BMI with a waist measurement: above 35 inches (88 cm) for women or 40 inches (102 cm) for men signals higher risk, mainly at a BMI of 25 to 34.9 (NHLBI).

The waist-to-height ratio is another simple check of fat distribution; a meta-analysis found it a better screening tool for cardiometabolic risk than waist circumference or BMI (Ashwell et al., 2012).

Limitation #3: Inaccurate for Athletes and Bodybuilders

At the NFL Scouting Combine, 53.4% of prospects had a BMI of 30 or more, but only 8.9% had obesity by measured body fat (Provencher et al., 2018).

For muscular people, a body fat estimate measures what BMI cannot. Learn more in our detailed guide: BMI for Athletes: Why Standard Measurements Don't Apply.

Limitation #4: Problems with Elderly Populations

Muscle mass decreases by about 3–8% per decade after age 30, and faster after 60; the involuntary loss of muscle mass, strength and function is called sarcopenia (Volpi et al., 2004). BMI cannot show that change, and NCHS notes that at a given BMI, body fat may vary by age (NCHS).

In a meta-analysis of adults aged 65 and over, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014). This is an observed association, not a target: the adult cut-offs stay the same at every age.

Limitation #5: Ethnic Variations in Body Composition

NCHS notes that health risks may begin at a lower BMI among Asian people, and that BMI may overestimate body fat among Black people at a given BMI compared with others (NCHS). In 2004, a WHO expert consultation found that a substantial share of Asian people have a high risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at BMIs below 25. It kept the international categories and identified BMI 23 and 27.5 as additional public-health action points. In the UK, NICE uses lower thresholds for adults with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background: overweight from 23 to 27.4 and obesity from 27.5 (NICE guideline NG246).

Limitation #6: Not Appropriate During Pregnancy

CDC’s weight-gain ranges for pregnancy go by BMI before pregnancy: for one baby, 28 to 40 lb below 18.5, 25 to 35 lb at 18.5 to 24.9, 15 to 25 lb at 25.0 to 29.9 and 11 to 20 lb at 30.0 to 39.9, from the Institute of Medicine (CDC).

Limitation #7: Misses "Skinny Fat" Condition (Normal Weight Obesity)

“Normal weight obesity”, or “skinny fat”, describes a healthy BMI with a high body fat percentage. BMI can’t flag it, because it does not directly measure body fat (CDC). Body fat varies widely at the same BMI: US women aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 23.5 to 24.4 measured a median of 35.5% body fat on CDC’s DXA scans, and the middle half were between 33.0% and 38.2% (our calculation from CDC NHANES 2011–2018 DXA scans). Limitation #8: Bone Density Not Considered

BMI counts bone along with fat and muscle (see Limitation #1), and bone differs between people: NIH contrasts slender, thin-boned women and men with larger-boned ones, who have more bone (NIAMS).

Limitation #9: Sex Differences Not Accounted For

CDC uses the same adult categories “regardless of age, sex, or race” (CDC), but women carry more fat at the same BMI: on CDC’s DXA scans of adults aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9, the median body fat was 33.4% for women and 22.1% for men (our calculation from CDC NHANES 2011–2018 DXA scans).

For sex-specific considerations, explore our dedicated guides: BMI for Women and BMI for Men.

Limitation #10: Doesn't Reflect Metabolic Health

BMI comes from height and weight alone, so it can’t show blood pressure, blood sugar or cholesterol. For individuals, CDC says BMI should be considered with other factors, such as blood pressure, cholesterol levels and a physical examination (CDC).

When BMI Is Still Useful

Where BMI still helps:

1

Population Studies

CDC’s survey has tracked adult obesity by measured BMI since 1960–1962: among adults aged 20 to 74, from 13.4% then to 40.8% in 2021–2023 (NCHS).

2

Initial Screening

Compared with direct measures of body fat, CDC lists BMI as quick and simple to calculate, inexpensive and noninvasive (CDC).

3

Tracking Changes

For the same person over time, BMI changes can indicate weight gain or loss trends.

4

Extreme Values

In pooled data from 239 studies, the risk of death rose at both ends: a hazard ratio of 1.51 at a BMI of 15.0 to 18.4 and 2.76 at 40.0 to 59.9, against 22.5 to 24.9 (GBMC, 2016).
