Underweight BMI Risks: Complete Guide to Health Concerns, Causes & Solutions

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

Key Takeaways
  • Definition: a BMI below 18.5 is underweight for adults (CDC); about 1.6% of US adults aged 20 and over were underweight in August 2021–August 2023 (NCHS).
  • Grades: WHO grades adult thinness in three steps: 17.0–18.4 (the calculator’s Underweight), 16.0–16.9 (moderate thinness) and below 16.0 (severe thinness) (WHO).
  • Risk of death: in pooled data from 239 studies, a BMI of 15.0 to 18.4 carried 1.51 times the risk of death of a BMI of 22.5 to 24.9 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).
  • Causes: poor nutrition and underlying health conditions, CDC’s survey team says (NCHS); weight lost without trying has a longer list, from depression to celiac disease (MedlinePlus).
  • When to act: MedlinePlus advises seeing a provider if you lose more than 10 pounds (4.5 kg), or 5% of your usual weight, within 6 to 12 months without knowing why (MedlinePlus).

What Defines Underweight BMI?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a screening measure worked out from your height and weight (CDC). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), a BMI below 18.5 kg/m2 is classified as underweight.

The BMI formula is simple: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). For example, a person who weighs 50 kg (110 lbs) and is 1.70 m (5'7") tall would have a BMI of 17.3, placing them in the underweight category.

Health Risks of Being Underweight

The Global BMI Mortality Collaboration pooled 239 studies from 32 countries and, to limit the effect of smoking and existing illness, analysed never-smokers without chronic disease at the start. Against a BMI of 22.5 to 24.9, a BMI of 15.0 to 18.4 carried 1.51 times the risk of death, close to the 1.45 of a BMI of 30.0 to 34.9 (Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, 2016).

For adults aged 65 and over, a meta-analysis of 197,940 people found that, compared with a BMI of 23 to 23.9, the risk of death was higher below 23 and began to rise at about 33 (Winter et al., 2014).

Osteoporosis and Bone Health

NIH lists body size among the risk factors for osteoporosis: slender, thin-boned women and men are at greater risk because they have less bone to lose. It adds that a diet low in calcium and vitamin D can raise the risk of osteoporosis and fractures, and that excessive dieting or poor protein intake may raise the risk of bone loss (NIAMS).

Fertility and Pregnancy

The HHS Office on Women’s Health lists being overweight or underweight among the things that can change a woman’s ability to have a baby. Most female infertility comes from problems with ovulation, and irregular or absent periods are one sign that ovulation isn’t normal (Office on Women’s Health).

For a pregnancy with one baby, CDC lists a weight gain of 28 to 40 lb for women who were underweight before pregnancy, against 25 to 35 lb for a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9; the ranges are the Institute of Medicine’s (CDC).

Causes of Being Underweight

CDC’s survey team names two broad causes: “Poor nutrition and underlying health conditions can result in underweight among youth and adults” (NCHS). For weight lost without trying, MedlinePlus lists a wider set (MedlinePlus):

  • Things that blunt appetite: depression, stress or anxiety; cancer, sometimes before other symptoms appear; long-term infections such as AIDS or tuberculosis; chronic diseases such as COPD or Parkinson disease; stimulant drugs such as cocaine and amphetamines; some medicines, among them chemotherapy and thyroid drugs.
  • Problems absorbing food: celiac disease; persistent diarrhea or infections such as parasites; long-term inflammation of the pancreas; surgery that removed part of the small intestine; laxative overuse.
  • Conditions not yet diagnosed: an eating disorder such as anorexia nervosa, diabetes, or an overactive thyroid.

Eating Disorders

NIMH describes eating disorders as serious illnesses, not choices, marked by severe disturbances in eating; people with them can be underweight, average weight or overweight, and those with avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) are often underweight (NIMH).

Signs of anorexia nervosa (NIMH)
  • Severely restricted eating
  • Intense, excessive exercise
  • Extreme thinness (emaciation)
  • A relentless drive to be thin and an unwillingness to keep a healthy weight
  • Intense fear of weight gain
  • A distorted body image or self-image
  • Denying how serious a low body weight is

Over time, NIMH says, anorexia nervosa can lead to thinning of the bones, mild anemia, muscle wasting and weakness, low blood pressure, slowed breathing and pulse, damage to the heart, feeling tired all the time and infertility, among other harms. People with any combination of these signs may have an eating disorder and should talk to a health care provider (NIMH).

Support: the Alliance for Eating Disorders runs a free helpline staffed by licensed therapists, 1-866-662-1235, Monday to Friday, 9:00 am to 7:00 pm Eastern (allianceforeatingdisorders.com). If you or someone you know is struggling or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, or chat at 988lifeline.org; in a life-threatening emergency, call 911 (NIMH).

When to See a Doctor

MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider in three situations (MedlinePlus):

  • You or someone in your family is losing more weight than is healthy for their age and height.
  • You have lost more than 10 pounds (4.5 kg), or 5% of your usual weight, within 6 to 12 months and don’t know why.
  • Other symptoms have come with the weight loss.

Expect a physical exam, a weight check and questions about your history and symptoms, and possibly a referral to a dietitian (MedlinePlus). If you have concerns about your eating behavior or mental health, NIMH says to talk to a primary care provider, who can refer you to a qualified mental health professional (NIMH).

Getting Help

The plan depends on the cause: MedlinePlus says any diet and exercise changes a provider suggests depend on what is behind the weight loss (MedlinePlus). When an eating disorder is the cause, NIMH says eating disorders can be treated successfully, and that early detection and treatment are important for recovery (NIMH). Mental Health Considerations

Low mood and worry can push weight down: MedlinePlus counts depression, stress and anxiety among the things that blunt appetite (MedlinePlus). Eating disorders carry their own risks: NIMH says people with them are at risk for co-occurring mental illnesses, most often depression, anxiety and substance use disorders, and are at a higher risk for suicide (NIMH).
