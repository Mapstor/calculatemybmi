Average Weight for Men and Women by Height and Age

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

What does the average American man weigh? What about a woman who is 5′4″, or a man who is 5′9″? And is the average a healthy weight?

The average US man weighs 199.0 lb and the average woman 171.8 lb, measured by CDC in 2021–2023. At every height with enough people measured, the average woman and the average man are above CDC’s healthy weight range for that height (our calculation).

Below: a tool that shows where your weight falls, the average at every height for women and men, the averages by age, height, BMI and waist size, and how average weight has changed since 1960.

Key takeaways

The average US man weighs 199.0 lb (90.3 kg) and the average woman 171.8 lb (77.9 kg): adults 20 and over, measured by CDC in August 2021–August 2023. At 5′4″ the average woman weighs 171.2 lb; at 5′9″ the average man weighs 204.0 lb (our calculation).

  • The average is above the healthy range at every height measured (women 4′9″–5′9″, men 5′2″–6′3″), and 60% to 78% of women and 64% to 81% of men of each height have a BMI of 25 or more.
  • Adults in their 30s weigh more than adults in their 20s: 13.4 lb more for women and 10.2 lb more for men. From 60 on, each older age group of women weighs less than the one before, and men 80 and over weigh 13.3 lb less than men in their 70s.
  • The average man is 68.9 inches tall and the average woman 63.5 inches; their average BMI is 29.4 and 30.0, and their average waist 40.6 and 38.5 inches (CDC).
  • Since 1960–1962, the average weight of men aged 20 to 74 has risen 33.9 lb and of women 33.5 lb (CDC; our calculation for 2021–2023).

Medical disclaimer: This page describes populations, not individuals. BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

Average weight by age

Women in their 30s weigh 13.4 lb more on average than women in their 20s, and men 10.2 lb more. From 30 to 59 the averages (177.3 to 180.0 lb for women, 201.5 to 210.1 lb for men) are not statistically different: the 95% confidence interval of the gap between any two of those age groups includes zero. From 60 on, each older age group of women weighs less than the one before, and men 80 and over weigh 13.3 lb less than men in their 70s. These are different people measured in the same years, not the same people followed as they aged.

Average height, BMI and waist size

The average US man is 68.9 inches tall (175.1 cm, about 5′9″) and the average woman 63.5 inches (161.2 cm, about 5′3.5″). Their average BMI is 29.4 and 30.0: in CDC’s overweight range for men, and at the line between overweight and obesity for women. A healthy BMI is 18.5 to 24.9 for adults of any age or sex (CDC). Half of men weigh 191.0 lb or more and half of women 162.0 lb or more; the medians sit below the averages because the heaviest people pull the averages up (NCHS).

The average waist is 40.6 inches for men and 38.5 inches for women (CDC); the middle half measure 36 to 44 and 33 to 43 inches. US guidelines treat a waist over 102 cm (40 in) in men and over 88 cm (35 in) in women as a sign of higher risk (NHLBI): 47.4% of men and 67.5% of women are over those lines (our calculation, in centimeters). The waist-to-height ratio guide shows how to measure.

Average weight since 1960

National health surveys have weighed Americans since 1960–1962. Men aged 20 to 74 averaged 166.3 lb in 1960–1962 and 191.0 lb in 1999–2002; women of the same ages 140.2 lb and 164.3 lb (CDC, Advance Data No. 347). In 2021–2023 men of those ages averaged 200.2 lb and women 173.7 lb (our calculation), 33.9 lb and 33.5 lb more than in 1960–1962.

Why some sites say 199.8 lb. That is CDC’s average for men 20 and over in 2015–2018, with 170.8 lb for women (NCHS Series 3 No. 46). CDC’s newest measurements, from August 2021 to August 2023, give 199.0 lb and 171.8 lb, the figures on its FastStats page. Both are averages over every height; for one height, use the tables above.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC NHANES August 2021–August 2023 demographics and body measures files (DEMO_L, BMX_L): our calculation of the average, percentiles and the share with a BMI of 25 or more at each height, of BMI and waist by age, and of the averages for ages 20 to 74 (2,688 men and 3,254 women aged 20 and over).
  • Fryar CD, et al. Anthropometric reference data for children and adults: United States, August 2021–August 2023. Vital and Health Statistics, Series 3, No. 50: Tables 4 (weight), 8 (height), 10 (BMI) and 12 (waist).
  • CDC. FastStats: Body Measurements: average height, weight and waist for adults 20 and over.
  • Fryar CD, et al. Anthropometric reference data for children and adults: United States, 2015–2018. Vital and Health Statistics, Series 3, No. 46 (January 2021).
  • Ogden CL, et al. Mean body weight, height, and body mass index, United States 1960–2002. Advance Data No. 347 (October 2004).
  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the adult cut-offs, the same for every adult.
  • NHLBI. Clinical Guidelines on the Identification, Evaluation, and Treatment of Overweight and Obesity in Adults, treatment guidelines chapter (NIH, 1998): waist cut-offs for women and men.

Method. Adults aged 20 and over in NHANES August 2021–August 2023, weighed and measured by CDC’s staff, weighted with the exam weights; pregnant women are left out of weight, BMI and waist, as NCHS leaves them out. Weights are converted to pounds with NCHS’s factor (2.2046 lb per kg), and percentiles read the weighted distribution over the measured values. With these rules our figures equal every mean and percentile in NCHS Series 3 No. 50: all 160 weight means and percentiles in Table 4, the adult height and waist means and percentiles in Tables 8 and 12, and the adult BMI means and medians in Table 10 (204 values). Heights for the tables are measured heights rounded to the nearest inch; a height is shown when 30 or more people were measured at it. 95% confidence intervals use Taylor linearization over the survey’s 15 strata, and the page states a change between age groups only when its interval excludes zero. The tool compares a weight with the people measured at the height entered or an inch either side, heights rounded to the nearest inch.
