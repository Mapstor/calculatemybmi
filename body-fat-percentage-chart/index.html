Body Fat Percentage Chart for Women and Men by Age

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm · Not medical advice

What body fat percentage is healthy for a woman or a man your age? Is 30% a lot? And how does body fat line up with BMI?

CDC’s adult categories use BMI, not body fat, and the body fat ranges many charts use are provisional. CDC’s body scans show what Americans actually measure: an average of 38.7% body fat for women and 27.2% for men aged 20 to 59 (our calculation, 2011–2018).

Below: the chart by age for women and men, where your number ranks, what every percentage means against US adults, and body fat at each BMI.

Key takeaways

Widely cited healthy body fat ranges are 21–32.9% for women and 8–19.9% for men aged 20 to 39, a few points higher at older ages. They are provisional ranges matched to a healthy BMI (Gallagher et al., 2000). US adults measure more on CDC’s scanner: an average of 38.7% for women and 27.2% for men aged 20 to 59.

  • Women measure about 11.5 points more than men on average, and 10.8 to 12.0 points more in every decade from 20 to 59 (our calculation from CDC body scans).
  • Body fat rises with age: the average goes up 2.9 points for men and 3.4 for women from their 20s to their 50s.
  • BMI is not a body fat reading: among US women with a healthy BMI, the middle half measured 29.8–36.5% body fat; among men, 18.6–25.0%.

Medical disclaimer: This page is for information only. Body fat percentage and BMI are measurements, not a diagnosis. Ask a healthcare professional for medical advice.

Where does your body fat rank?

Enter a reading from a scan, a scale or calipers. The tool compares it with US women or men of your age group in CDC’s body scans (or girls and boys from age 8) and shows the provisional range for your age. A scale or calipers can read differently from a DXA scan: in a study of 437 college students, a hand-held impedance device was 4.9 points off on average (Burns et al., 2019; how to measure body fat).

Healthy body fat percentage

CDC’s adult categories use BMI, and CDC notes that “BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass” (CDC).

The ranges in the chart come from Gallagher and colleagues (Am J Clin Nutr, 2000). They measured body fat in 1,626 adults at three universities, Cambridge in the UK, Columbia in the US and Jikei in Japan, with DXA scans at all three and a four-compartment model at two. From that they predicted the body fat that goes with a BMI of 18.5, 25 and 30 by sex, age and ethnic group (white, African American and Asian adults), and called the ranges provisional.

Many charts online add tiers such as “athletes” and “fitness”. Those tiers come from a fitness organization’s chart and aren’t matched to BMI or age, so this page leaves them out.

How body fat is measured matters too. CDC’s scanner software adds 5% of lean mass to the fat mass (CDC), and every US figure on this page, the tables above included, comes from those scans. So compare a reading with numbers from the same kind of measurement where you can.

Body fat and BMI

On average, body fat rises with BMI, but in CDC’s scans of US adults aged 20 to 59, people with the same BMI measured a wide spread of body fat. At every BMI from 19 to 30, the median woman measured about 11 to 13 points more than the median man.

How the provisional ranges line up with CDC’s scans

The provisional ranges match a BMI of 25 to the start of “high”: 20% body fat for men and 33% for women aged 20 to 39, as tabulated in J Public Health Afr 2016. In CDC’s scans, adults within half a point of a BMI of 25 averaged 24.3% (men) and 35.6% (women) at that age. So on CDC’s scanner, readings in the provisional “high” range are common at a healthy BMI: 60.6% of US men and 49.1% of US women aged 20 to 59 with a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 measured in the “high” range or above (our calculation).

Body fat by age and sex

Age. From their 20s to their 50s, the average rose 2.9 points for US men (25.4% to 28.3%) and 3.4 points for women (36.9% to 40.3%).

Sex. Women measured 10.8 to 12.0 points more body fat than men in every decade from 20 to 59 (our calculation from CDC body scans).

Children and teens. Boys’ average fell from 29.2% at 8 to 11 to 23.4% at 16 to 19, while girls’ rose from 32.5% to 35.3%. Under 20, BMI is read as a percentile for age and sex (CDC); the BMI calculator for children and teens works it out.

Over 60. CDC’s 2011–2018 scans stopped at age 59, so there are no US measurements for older adults here; the chart’s 60 to 79 rows are the provisional ranges only.

Frequently asked questions

Widely cited ranges are provisional ones matched to a healthy BMI (Gallagher et al., 2000): for women, 21–32.9% at ages 20 to 39, 23–33.9% at 40 to 59 and 24–35.9% at 60 to 79; for men, 8–19.9% at 20 to 39, 11–21.9% at 40 to 59 and 13–24.9% at 60 to 79. What US adults measure is higher: an average of 38.7% for women and 27.2% for men aged 20 to 59 (our calculation from CDC body scans, 2011–2018).

CDC’s scans show how low US women measure: of 5,462 women aged 20 to 59, 1 in 100 measured under 23.3% body fat and 1 in 20 under 27.2% (our calculation, 2011–2018). On the provisional ranges, body fat under 21% at ages 20 to 39 (under 23% at 40 to 59) is “low”: the level that goes with an underweight BMI (Gallagher et al., 2000).

It depends on sex. For a woman, 30% is inside the provisional healthy range at 20 to 39 (21–32.9%), and 85% of US women that age measured more. For a man, 30% is above the range at every age: “very high” starts at 25% at 20 to 39, 28% at 40 to 59 and 30% at 60 to 79 (Gallagher et al., 2000). Still, 29% of US men aged 20 to 39 measured more than 30% (our calculation from CDC body scans).

Not for a man: 20% is the start of the provisional “high” range at 20 to 39 and inside the healthy range at 40 to 59 (11–21.9%), and 82% of US men aged 20 to 39 measured more. For a woman, 20% is under the provisional healthy range (it starts at 21% at 20 to 39), and more than 99% of US women aged 20 to 39 measured more (our calculation from CDC body scans).

“Body fat index” isn’t one fixed measure. If you mean body fat percentage, the share of body weight that is fat, the provisional healthy ranges are 21–32.9% for women and 8–19.9% for men aged 20 to 39, a little higher with age (Gallagher et al., 2000). If you mean the body mass index (BMI), weight divided by height squared, the healthy range is 18.5 to 24.9 (CDC). There is also a fat mass index: fat mass in kilograms divided by height in meters squared.

Sources and method

Written by Marko Visic, MPharm. “Our calculation” means we computed the figure from CDC’s public survey files with the method below.

  • CDC NHANES 2011–2012 to 2017–2018 whole-body DXA scans (DXX_G to DXX_J) with the demographics and body measures files: our calculation for 10,864 adults aged 20 to 59 (5,462 women, 5,402 men) and for children and teens from 8. CDC’s documentation: ages 8 to 59; pregnancy, a weight over 450 lb or a height over 6′5″ exclude a scan; valid scans are less common at higher BMIs; the software adds 5% of lean mass to the fat mass.
  • Liu B, et al. Trends in obesity and adiposity measures by race or ethnicity among adults in the United States 2011-18 (BMJ 2021;372:n365): our method reproduces its mean body fat for every survey cycle.
  • Gallagher D, et al. Healthy percentage body fat ranges: an approach for developing guidelines based on body mass index (Am J Clin Nutr 2000;72:694–701): the provisional ranges.
  • Interpreting the body fat percentage result, Table 1 (J Public Health Afr 2016;7:515): the provisional ranges as tabulated.
  • CDC. About Body Mass Index (BMI): what BMI does and doesn’t measure.
  • CDC. Adult BMI Categories: the healthy range of 18.5 to 24.9.
  • CDC. Calculating BMI: the formula in US units.
  • CDC. Child and Teen BMI Categories: percentiles for ages 2 to 19.
  • Burns RD, Fu Y, Constantino N. Measurement agreement in percent body fat estimates among laboratory and field assessments in college students: use of equivalence testing (PLOS ONE 2019): how far home methods read from DXA (437 college students).

Method. We pooled CDC’s National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles 2011–2012 through 2017–2018, kept valid whole-body scans, left out pregnant women and used CDC’s exam weights divided by four, as CDC’s guidelines advise for four cycles. Percentiles are weighted. Shares above or below a percentage count readings strictly above or below it, and every figure is rounded once, from the unrounded estimate. We tested averages and differences with 95% confidence intervals from Taylor linearization over the survey’s strata and primary sampling units (62 degrees of freedom); the page states only differences whose interval excludes zero, and the CSV download gives each average’s standard error. Body fat at each BMI uses the measured BMI as CDC records it. Because valid scans are less common at higher BMIs, the heaviest adults are under-represented and the averages may run a little low. With the same age adjustment as Liu et al. (BMJ 2021), our figures reproduce their mean body fat for every cycle from the same 10,864 adults.
